The One Who Came Back

Luke 17:11–19 · 10 min read

By Dr. Davies Rene Segera · 2026-10-24

Jesus heals ten men, and one returns to thank Him. Reflect on the grateful Samaritan, remembering God’s goodness, and responding to freely given grace with praise and gratitude.

Key Verse
“Didn’t I heal ten men? Where are the other nine?”
Luke 17:17, NLT, excerpt

Most of us know how to pray when trouble comes. A troubling diagnosis or a child in danger can bring us to God in earnest prayer. But what happens when the answer comes? A brief account in Luke’s Gospel gently invites us to consider what we do next.

Jesus was making His final journey toward Jerusalem, where He would give His life. Luke writes: “As Jesus continued on toward Jerusalem, he reached the border between Galilee and Samaria. As he entered a village there, ten men with leprosy stood at a distance, crying out, ‘Jesus, Master, have mercy on us!’” Luke 17:11–13, NLT

In biblical times, the word translated “leprosy” referred to a range of serious skin diseases. To protect the community, the Law of Moses required those affected to live apart from others: “They must cover their mouth and call out, ‘Unclean! Unclean!’ As long as the serious disease lasts, they will be ceremonially unclean. They must live in isolation in their place outside the camp.” Leviticus 13:45–46, NLT, excerpt Being “ceremonially unclean” meant being cut off from public worship, home, and the embrace of loved ones. These men stood at a distance and cried out for mercy.

Ellen G. White notes that shared suffering had drawn together people who would ordinarily have kept apart. She uses “malady,” an older word for illness: “These lepers were made up of Jews and Samaritans, and the prejudice that existed between them was broken down by this terrible malady, and, doomed to death, they associated together.” The Signs of the Times, June 25, 1896, par. 2, excerpt Luke tells us the background of only one man; Ellen G. White describes the group as a whole. Whatever their backgrounds, all ten shared the same need.

Each of us comes to Jesus in need of His mercy.

Jesus’ reply may have surprised them: “He looked at them and said, ‘Go show yourselves to the priests.’ And as they went, they were cleansed of their leprosy.” Luke 17:14, NLT The Law required anyone who had recovered to take this step: “Those who have been healed must be brought to the priest” Leviticus 14:2, NLT, excerpt Yet Jesus sent them on their way before they could see any change in their skin.

All ten began the journey. Ellen G. White writes: “Their faith had been tested by Christ’s direction; but they did not defer a moment to act upon his word.” The Signs of the Times, June 25, 1896, par. 4, excerpt Each man trusted Jesus enough to take the first step, and all ten were healed as they went. Nothing in Luke’s account suggests that the nine were insincere. They asked for mercy, obeyed Jesus’ command, and received the healing they sought.

The nine received God’s gift. What they missed was returning to the One who gave it.

Luke then draws our attention to one man: “One of them, when he saw that he was healed, came back to Jesus, shouting, ‘Praise God!’ He fell to the ground at Jesus’ feet, thanking him for what he had done. This man was a Samaritan.” Luke 17:15–16, NLT

For centuries, Jews and Samaritans had been divided over history and worship. Just a few chapters earlier, a Samaritan village had refused to welcome Jesus: “But the people of the village did not welcome Jesus because he was on his way to Jerusalem.” Luke 9:53, NLT Yet here, it was a Samaritan who came back.

Jesus noticed the man who returned, and He also noticed the nine who did not: “Jesus asked, ‘Didn’t I heal ten men? Where are the other nine? Has no one returned to give glory to God except this foreigner?’” Luke 17:17–18, NLT Ellen G. White describes what set this outsider apart. Here, “susceptible” means open and responsive:

“This Samaritan believed in Christ as the Redeemer of both soul and body. He made manifest the fact that he was more susceptible to the grace of God, and more appreciative of divine love, than were the other nine.”The Signs of the Times, June 25, 1896, par. 5, excerpt

Jesus then spoke directly to the man: “And Jesus said to the man, ‘Stand up and go. Your faith has healed you.’” Luke 17:19, NLT The NLT notes that these words can also be translated, “Your faith has saved you.” All ten had been cleansed, but only the man who returned heard these words from Jesus. Many readers understand this as a blessing that reaches beyond physical healing. Luke does not tell us what became of the nine, so we need not speculate.

Gratitude brought this man back to Jesus’ feet, where Jesus spoke to him personally.

It is easy to judge the nine until we recognize something of ourselves in their story. Luke does not tell us why they failed to return. Yet we know how earnestly we can pray in a crisis, only to move on once it has passed. Ellen G. White describes the same pattern, using “bade,” an older word for “told”:

“When the ten lepers came to Jesus for healing, He bade them go and show themselves to the priest. On the way they were cleansed, but only one of them returned to give Him glory. The others went their way, forgetting Him who had made them whole. How many are still doing the same thing!”The Desire of Ages, p. 348 · DA 348.1, excerpt

Later in the same paragraph, she explains why ingratitude matters: “He has given all the riches of heaven to redeem them, and yet they are unmindful of His great love. By their ingratitude they close their hearts against the grace of God.” The Desire of Ages, p. 348 · DA 348.1, excerpt Ingratitude does not change God’s goodness. It can, however, dull our awareness of His presence and make us less open to what He wants to give us.

David gives us words to resist such forgetfulness: “Let all that I am praise the Lord; may I never forget the good things he does for me.” Psalm 103:2, NLT Remembering takes intention. We need to pause, look back, and name what God has done.

Gratitude grows when we choose to remember what God has done.

Consider, too, that Jesus healed all ten, even though only one returned. Ellen G. White points to His generosity: “For the sake of this one man, who would make a right use of the blessing of health, Jesus healed the whole ten.” Testimonies for the Church, vol. 3, p. 180 · 3T 180.1, excerpt Jesus did not wait for proof of gratitude before showing mercy. He was on His way to Jerusalem to give Himself for people who would so often forget Him.

Our gratitude can never repay His grace. Paul writes: “God saved you by his grace when you believed. And you can’t take credit for this; it is a gift from God. Salvation is not a reward for the good things we have done, so none of us can boast about it.” Ephesians 2:8–9, NLT Grace comes first; gratitude is our response to a gift already given.

What might returning to Jesus look like in our daily lives? Ellen G. White offers this practical counsel: “It is for our own benefit to keep every gift of God fresh in our memory. Thus faith is strengthened to claim and to receive more and more.” The Desire of Ages, p. 348 · DA 348.2, excerpt She goes on to compare this practice with the memorial stones Israel set up to remember God’s help:

“Like the people of Israel, let us set up our stones of witness, and inscribe upon them the precious story of what God has wrought for us.”The Desire of Ages, p. 348 · DA 348.2, excerpt

Our “stones of witness” can be simple: a list of answered prayers or a pause to give thanks before we bring another request. The psalmist gives us a question to make our own: “What can I offer the Lord for all he has done for me?” Psalm 116:12, NLT

Today, remember one specific way God has helped you. Before the day is over, come back to Jesus and thank Him for it. Then share that story with someone else.

Remember what Jesus has done. Come back to Him with thanks, and tell others about His goodness.

Reflect

  1. When God has answered my prayers, have I returned to thank Him, or simply carried on with my life?
  2. What “stone of witness” could I set up this week to remember a specific way God has helped me?
  3. What can I learn from the faith and gratitude of the “outsiders” around me?

Key Takeaways

  1. Jesus notices who comes back. All ten received healing, but only one returned to give glory to God and thank Jesus. Luke 17:17–18 · quoted above
  2. Gratitude is a choice to remember. As we keep God’s goodness fresh in our memory, our faith grows stronger. Psalm 103:2 · quoted above
  3. Grace comes first; gratitude responds. Jesus gives freely, even to those who forget Him. We give thanks for a gift we could never earn. Ephesians 2:8–9 · quoted above

A prayer for today

Lord Jesus, thank You for hearing our cries for mercy. Forgive us for the times we have received Your gifts and carried on without returning to thank You. Open our hearts to Your grace, as You opened the heart of the man who returned. Teach us to remember Your goodness, praise You with joy, and tell others what You have done. Thank You for giving Yourself freely for us, before we ever thought to thank You. In Your name we pray. Amen.

— Dr. Davies Rene Segera
