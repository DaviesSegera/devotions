Today's Devotion

When Envy Takes Root

Genesis 37 · 10 min read
Key Verse
“But his brothers hated Joseph because their father loved him more than the rest of them. They couldn’t say a kind word to him.”
Genesis 37:4 (NLT)

It usually begins with something that was never your doing. “Jacob loved Joseph more than any of his other children because Joseph had been born to him in his old age. So one day Jacob had a special gift made for Joseph—a beautiful robe” Genesis 37:3 (NLT). Ten older sons watched their father’s preference arrive in the shape of a garment they would never be given. Notice where the fault actually lies: the favouritism was Jacob’s, not Joseph’s, and Joseph did not ask to be preferred.

Ellen White is unsparing about the father — the robe was “the father’s injudicious gift,” a coat “such as was usually worn by persons of distinction,” which “seemed to them another evidence of his partiality.” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 209 · PP 209.4. And then comes the line that names what grew in the shade of it: “But his brothers hated Joseph because their father loved him more than the rest of them. They couldn’t say a kind word to him” Genesis 37:4 (NLT).

That last sentence is the tell. Envy almost never announces itself. Its first symptom is a mouth gone dry toward one particular person — the compliment you could give and don’t, the good news you hear and do not repeat.

Then watch what feeds it. “One night Joseph had a dream, and when he told his brothers about it, they hated him more than ever” Genesis 37:5 (NLT). He tells them the sheaves bowed; they answer, “So you think you will be our king, do you?” — “And they hated him all the more because of his dreams and the way he talked about them” Genesis 37:8 (NLT). He tells them the sun, moon and eleven stars bowed; his father scolds him for it; and Scripture closes the scene with its own quiet bookkeeping: “But while his brothers were jealous of Joseph, his father wondered what the dreams meant” Genesis 37:11 (NLT).

Hated. Hated more than ever. Hated all the more. Jealous. Read those four steps again and look for the dramatic event that caused them. There isn’t one. Nothing happened between those verses except time and a few words. Ellen White marks the same slow feeding: “Their malice was still further increased as the boy one day told them of a dream that he had had.” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 209 · PP 209.4. A root does not need much — a little soil, a little dark, and no one to pull it.

And notice the two men who heard exactly the same words. Jacob “wondered what the dreams meant”; his sons “were jealous” Genesis 37:11 (NLT). Same information, different soil. That is worth sitting with, because it says the problem was never really Joseph. Envy is also strangely particular about what it hates: not what is wrong with a person, but what is right with them. Ellen White describes the boy his brothers could not stand — “Pure, active, and joyous, the lad gave evidence also of moral earnestness and firmness”; “the qualities that afterward distinguished him in Egypt—gentleness, fidelity, and truthfulness—were already manifest.” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 209 · PP 209.1.

Centuries later a Roman governor looked at a crowd of religious leaders and read them at a glance: “He knew very well that the religious leaders had arrested Jesus out of envy” Matthew 27:18 (NLT). Even Pilate could see it. Envy is the one sin that carries no pleasure with it at all — the greedy man at least enjoys his money — which is why Scripture calls it what it calls it: “A peaceful heart leads to a healthy body; jealousy is like cancer in the bones” Proverbs 14:30 (NLT).

Be fair to the brothers on one point, though, because it protects us from a lazy reading. Joseph was seventeen and not faultless: he “reported to his father some of the bad things his brothers were doing” Genesis 37:2 (NLT), and after the first dream landed badly he told them the second one anyway. His own father asked him, “What kind of dream is that?” Genesis 37:10 (NLT). There is real wisdom to learn about when and how to speak of what God has given you.

But hold the two things apart: Joseph’s immaturity was the occasion of their envy, never its cause. Envy always finds an occasion. If it had not been the robe, it would have been the dreams; if not the dreams, the way he walked. No one else’s behaviour puts a root in your heart.

Now come to the harvest, and count the years it took to ripen. “When Joseph’s brothers saw him coming, they recognized him in the distance. As he approached, they made plans to kill him” Genesis 37:18 (NLT). “Here comes the dreamer!” Genesis 37:19 (NLT) — the very word they had once sneered at is now the word they kill by. They stripped off the robe Genesis 37:23, threw him into an empty cistern, and then this: “Then, just as they were sitting down to eat, they looked up and saw a caravan of camels in the distance” Genesis 37:25 (NLT).

They sat down to eat. Their brother was in a hole in the ground and they laid out a meal. In the end they sold him “for twenty pieces of silver” Genesis 37:28 (NLT). Ellen White names precisely what was in charge by then: “Envy and revenge, long secretly cherished, now controlled them,” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 210 · PP 210.6 and “the same spirit that actuated Cain was kindling in their hearts.” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 210 · PP 210.3. Read those four words again — long secretly cherished.

Not one of those ten men woke up that morning intending to sell a brother. That is exactly what roots do: by the time the moment arrives, the unthinkable already feels reasonable. “For wherever there is jealousy and selfish ambition, there you will find disorder and evil of every kind” James 3:16 (NLT).

And what did they gain? Twenty pieces of silver, divided ten ways. What it cost them was a father who refused to be comforted, a lie that had to be kept alive at the family table for more than twenty years, and a secret they carried in their own bodies — Ellen White says they “still hid in their own hearts the knowledge of their guilt.” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 212 · PP 212.1. Years later, standing frightened in an Egyptian court, they finally said it out loud to one another: they were guilty concerning their brother Genesis 42:21.

That is envy’s arithmetic. It promises to level the ground and only ever digs a hole, and the person who falls into it first is the one holding the spade. “Anger is cruel, and wrath is like a flood, but jealousy is even more dangerous” Proverbs 27:4 (NLT). So the New Testament tells us to go after it at ground level, early, while it is still small enough to pull: “Watch out that no poisonous root of bitterness grows up to trouble you, corrupting many” Hebrews 12:15 (NLT).

Three honest things help. Name it — envy survives by being called something more respectable, like discernment, or concern, or just being realistic; you know it is envy when one particular person’s good news tastes bad. Starve it — “Be happy with those who are happy” Romans 12:15 (NLT), deliberately and out loud, before you feel like it, because “Love is not jealous” 1 Corinthians 13:4 (NLT). And take the ache to God as grief rather than as a grudge — underneath a great deal of envy is a genuine loss, the child or the marriage or the health or the work you wanted and do not have.

That sorrow belongs in prayer. It does not belong in your heart as a case against the person who has what you wanted.

But suppose it is already grown. Suppose you are one of the ten, or suppose you are the one they sold. The last chapter of Genesis holds both. Their father is dead, and the brothers panic: “Now Joseph will show his anger and pay us back for all the wrong we did to him” Genesis 50:15 (NLT). They send a message begging him to forgive their sin, and “when Joseph received the message, he broke down and wept” Genesis 50:17 (NLT) — wept, surely, because after all those years they still believed he was keeping accounts.

Then: “Don’t be afraid of me. Am I God, that I can punish you?” Genesis 50:19 (NLT). “You intended to harm me, but God intended it all for good. He brought me to this position so I could save the lives of many people” Genesis 50:20 (NLT). “No, don’t be afraid. I will continue to take care of you and your children” Genesis 50:21 (NLT). Look closely at what verse 20 does not do. It does not rename their sin or make it smaller.

Joseph says it plainly — you intended to harm me — and only then says what God did with it. Ellen White keeps the same line: “Joseph, through his bondage in Egypt, became a savior to his father’s family; yet this fact did not lessen the guilt of his brothers.” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 239 · PP 239.3. A good outcome never launders a wrong. And that matters pastorally: God worked it for good is a sentence for the wounded person to say in their own time, in God’s company — never a sentence anyone else may use to hurry you past what was done to you, to excuse the one who did it, or to keep you within reach of someone who is still harming you.

If you are in danger, get to safety and get help. That is wisdom, not a failure of faith.

So do the small, unglamorous thing this morning: go and look at the soil. Is there a name that tightens your jaw? Someone whose good news you have to work at celebrating? A kind word you have been withholding for so long that the silence now feels normal? Pull it up today, while it is still a seedling and not yet a system. And if it has already grown — if you are one of the brothers, or the one who was sold — the God of Joseph still comes down to the roots.

Ellen White saw whose face was in that Egyptian room: “Joseph’s patience and meekness under injustice and oppression, his ready forgiveness and noble benevolence toward his unnatural brothers, represent the Saviour’s uncomplaining endurance.” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 239 · PP 239.4. There is a robe in this story that was torn, and there is a Brother who was sold, and He is not keeping accounts either.

Envy never stays small — so pull it up while it is small.

Key Takeaways

  1. Envy’s first symptom is a mouth gone dry — they couldn’t say a kind word to him. Long before anyone plots anything, the kindness quietly stops.
  2. The soil is usually something that is nobody’s fault but the giver’s — the robe was Jacob’s idea, not Joseph’s; Ellen White calls it the father’s injudicious gift.
  3. Roots grow on nothing but time and silence — hated, then hated him more than ever, then hated him all the more, then jealous. Look for the dramatic event in between; there isn’t one.
  4. Envy hates what is right in a person, not what is wrong — the brothers could not stand a boy marked by gentleness, fidelity, and truthfulness, and even Pilate could see that Jesus was handed over out of envy.
  5. The occasion is never the cause — Joseph’s tale-bearing and his timing were immature, and there is wisdom to learn there; but no one else’s behaviour puts a root in your heart.
  6. By the time the moment came, the unthinkable felt reasonable — envy and revenge, long secretly cherished, now controlled them. They threw their brother in a pit and then sat down to eat.
  7. Grace reaches the root, but it never renames the sin — you intended to harm me, but God intended it all for good. God’s good outcome is not a verdict that the wrong was small, and mercy never means staying where you are being harmed.
