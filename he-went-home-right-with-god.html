He Went Home Right With God

Luke 18:9–14 · 10 min read

By Dr. Davies Rene Segera · 2026-11-26

Two men go to the Temple to pray. One measures himself against others, while the tax collector asks for mercy and goes home right with God. Reflect on humility, honest prayer, and trusting Christ’s grace.

PrayerHumility & obedienceForgiveness & grace

Key Verse
“But the tax collector stood at a distance and dared not even lift his eyes to heaven as he prayed. Instead, he beat his chest in sorrow, saying, ‘O God, be merciful to me, for I am a sinner.’”
Luke 18:13 (NLT)

Have you ever felt a little better about yourself after noticing someone else’s faults? Jesus shows us where that habit can lead in a story about two men who went to the Temple to pray.

Luke tells us whom Jesus had in mind. “Then Jesus told this story to some who had great confidence in their own righteousness and scorned everyone else:” Luke 18:9, NLT Their confidence showed in the way they looked at other people.

“Two men went to the Temple to pray. One was a Pharisee, and the other was a despised tax collector.” Luke 18:10, NLT Pharisees were respected for their devotion to God’s law. Paul recalled his own years as a Pharisee when he wrote, “I was a member of the Pharisees, who demand the strictest obedience to the Jewish law.” Philippians 3:5, NLT, excerpt

Tax collectors worked for Rome, and many took more than people owed. Ellen G. White writes, “Of the Roman officials in Palestine, none were more hated than the publicans.” The Desire of Ages, p. 272 · DA 272.1, excerpt “Publican” is an older English term for a tax collector.

Both men came to the same place to pray. Only one knew how much he needed God.

The Pharisee prayed first. “The Pharisee stood by himself and prayed this prayer: ‘I thank you, God, that I am not like other people—cheaters, sinners, adulterers. I’m certainly not like that tax collector!” Luke 18:11, NLT “I fast twice a week, and I give you a tenth of my income.’” Luke 18:12, NLT

What he said about himself may well have been true. He fasted more often than the law required and gave a tenth of all he received. He trusted those deeds to make him right with God. How did he decide he had done enough? Ellen G. White writes,

“He judges his character, not by the holy character of God, but by the character of other men. His mind is turned away from God to humanity. This is the secret of his self-satisfaction.”Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 150 · COL 150.3, excerpt

Notice what is missing from his prayer. He thanks God and asks Him for nothing. As Ellen G. White wrote of his prayer, “He made no supplication; he expressed no want.” The Signs of the Times, February 19, 1885 · ST Feb. 19, 1885, par. 4, excerpt A supplication is a humble request.

Once he took himself as the measure of a good man, what could he see in the tax collector except his faults? “Whoever trusts in himself that he is righteous, will despise others. As the Pharisee judges himself by other men, so he judges other men by himself.” Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 151 · COL 151.2, excerpt Paul reminds us that God’s standard applies to all of us. “For everyone has sinned; we all fall short of God’s glorious standard.” Romans 3:23, NLT

The Pharisee could look at his neighbor and feel righteous. In the light of God’s holiness, we can all see our need for mercy.

Now consider the man the Pharisee looked down on. “But the tax collector stood at a distance and dared not even lift his eyes to heaven as he prayed. Instead, he beat his chest in sorrow, saying, ‘O God, be merciful to me, for I am a sinner.’” Luke 18:13, NLT

The Pharisee had spoken about him, yet his own prayer made no mention of the Pharisee. He had come to speak to God about his own sin. Ellen G. White writes,

“He did not compare himself with others. Overwhelmed with a sense of guilt, he stood as if alone in God’s presence. His only desire was for pardon and peace, his only plea was the mercy of God. And he was blessed.”Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 151 · COL 151.3, excerpt

His sorrow echoes David’s prayer after his own sin. “The sacrifice you desire is a broken spirit. You will not reject a broken and repentant heart, O God.” Psalm 51:17, NLT

The tax collector offered God no list of good deeds. His hope began when he recognized his need for mercy. “The sense of need, the recognition of our poverty and sin, is the very first condition of acceptance with God.” Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 152 · COL 152.1, excerpt On the mountainside, Jesus spoke of that same need. “God blesses those who are poor and realize their need for him, for the Kingdom of Heaven is theirs.” Matthew 5:3, NLT

He came with empty hands and asked for mercy, a gift none of us can earn.

Which of these two men went home right with God? Jesus gives His answer. “I tell you, this sinner, not the Pharisee, returned home justified before God. For those who exalt themselves will be humbled, and those who humble themselves will be exalted.” Luke 18:14, NLT

To be justified is to be made right with God. Christians explain that word in different ways. Here we see what it meant for these two men. The Pharisee went home still trusting his own record. The tax collector asked God for mercy and went home forgiven. Paul describes that gift in his letter to the Romans. “Yet God, in his grace, freely makes us right in his sight. He did this through Christ Jesus when he freed us from the penalty for our sins.” Romans 3:24, NLT

Ellen G. White sums up the Pharisee’s experience in a few words. “He felt no need, and he received nothing.” Steps to Christ, p. 30 · SC 30.2, excerpt

Jesus closes the parable with a promise for anyone who comes to God in humility. James echoes His words. “Humble yourselves before the Lord, and he will lift you up in honor.” James 4:10, NLT

The man who claimed nothing went home with what only God can give.

It is easy to read this story and think, “At least I am not like that Pharisee.” With that thought, we have already begun to pray his prayer. Ellen G. White describes how quietly pride can enter even our prayers for humility.

“The lips may express a poverty of soul that the heart does not acknowledge. While speaking to God of poverty of spirit, the heart may be swelling with the conceit of its own superior humility and exalted righteousness. In one way only can a true knowledge of self be obtained. We must behold Christ.”Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 159 · COL 159.1, excerpt

To behold Jesus is to give Him our full attention. When we catch ourselves dwelling on someone else’s faults, we can turn our eyes to Him.

The mercy the tax collector asked for comes to us through Jesus. He became like us “so that he could be our merciful and faithful High Priest before God. Then he could offer a sacrifice that would take away the sins of the people.” Hebrews 2:17, NLT, excerpt Because of Him, we can draw near to God. “So let us come boldly to the throne of our gracious God. There we will receive his mercy, and we will find grace to help us when we need it most.” Hebrews 4:16, NLT

Ellen G. White adds, “He will look upon no trembling suppliant without raising him up.” Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 157 · COL 157.2, excerpt A suppliant is someone who humbly asks for help.

When we look at Jesus, we stop comparing ourselves with others and find the courage to ask for mercy.

Christians in many traditions have made the tax collector’s words their own. Many Eastern Orthodox Christians draw on this verse when they pray, “Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me, a sinner.” Perhaps you have kept your distance from God because you feel you must first become a better person. Ellen G. White writes, “If you see your sinfulness, do not wait to make yourself better.” Steps to Christ, p. 31 · SC 31.1, excerpt Or perhaps, like the Pharisee, you have quietly judged someone else. Whatever you need to confess, you can trust God’s promise. “But if we confess our sins to him, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all wickedness.” 1 John 1:9, NLT

We cannot make our own hearts humble. When we find it hard even to pray for humility, Ellen G. White gives us words we can borrow.

“Then the language of the soul will be, Lord, take my heart; for I cannot give it. It is Thy property. Keep it pure, for I cannot keep it for Thee.”Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 159 · COL 159.3, excerpt

There is joy in receiving God’s mercy. “But Christ dwelling in the soul is a wellspring of joy.” Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 162 · COL 162.1, excerpt The tax collector walked into the Temple carrying his guilt. He walked home forgiven.

Come as you are, ask for His mercy, and go home right with God.

Reflect

  1. When I pray, is my attention on God, or am I measuring myself against someone else?
  2. Is there someone I quietly look down on? How might God want me to see that person?
  3. What would it mean for me to come to God this week with nothing but my need?

Key Takeaways

  1. Comparison hides our need. The Pharisee measured himself by other people and asked God for nothing. Luke 18:11–12 · quoted above
  2. God hears the honest cry for mercy. The tax collector compared himself with no one and pleaded for mercy alone. Luke 18:13 · quoted above
  3. God lifts up the humble. The one who asked for mercy went home right with God. That mercy reaches us through Jesus, our merciful High Priest. Luke 18:14 · quoted above

A prayer for today

Merciful God, we come to You as the tax collector did, with nothing to offer but our need. Forgive us for measuring ourselves against others and looking down on them. When we catch ourselves making those comparisons, turn our eyes to Jesus, our merciful High Priest. Help us trust Your grace. Lord Jesus, we cannot make our hearts humble by ourselves. Take our hearts, keep them, and fill them with Your joy. Have mercy on us today, and send us home right with You. In Jesus’ name, amen.

— Dr. Davies Rene Segera
