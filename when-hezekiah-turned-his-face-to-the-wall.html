When Hezekiah Turned His Face to the Wall

2 Kings 20 · 7 min read

By Dr. Davies Rene Segera

Hezekiah turns to God in 2 Kings 20. Reflect on honest prayer, dependence, and bringing frightening news into God’s presence.

PrayerHope & encouragement
Watch the Short

Key Verse
“When Hezekiah heard this, he turned his face to the wall and prayed to the Lord.”
2 Kings 20:2 (NLT)
“I have heard your prayer and seen your tears.”
2 Kings 20:5 (NLT, excerpt)

What do you do when the news before you is more than you know how to carry? 2 Kings 20 opens in a sickroom. “About that time Hezekiah became deathly ill, and the prophet Isaiah son of Amoz went to visit him. He gave the king this message: ‘This is what the Lord says: Set your affairs in order, for you are going to die. You will not recover from this illness.’” 2 Kings 20:1 (NLT) Hezekiah, king of Judah, was a faithful king, and that did not spare him this day.

Isaiah was not offering an opinion; he carried the word of the Lord. Ellen G. White describes the king’s position:

“In the midst of his prosperous reign King Hezekiah was suddenly stricken with a fatal malady. ‘Sick unto death,’ his case was beyond the power of man to help. And the last vestige of hope seemed removed when the prophet Isaiah appeared before him.” Prophets and Kings, p. 340 · PK 340.1

A crown could not remove Hezekiah’s vulnerability.

Here is the verse behind our title. “When Hezekiah heard this, he turned his face to the wall and prayed to the Lord, ‘Remember, O Lord, how I have always been faithful to you and have served you single-mindedly, always doing what pleases you.’ Then he broke down and wept bitterly.” 2 Kings 20:2–3 (NLT) Scripture records the turn to the wall; it does not explain it. What it makes plain is where the prayer went. The power was not in the wall; Hezekiah’s prayer was directed to the Lord.

After describing the king’s desperate condition, Ellen G. White directs our attention to the possibility that remained open to him: “The outlook seemed utterly dark; yet the king could still pray to the One who had hitherto been his ‘refuge and strength, a very present help in trouble.’ Psalm 46:1.” Prophets and Kings, p. 340 · PK 340.2 He did not ask for fifteen years, and he did not bargain; he appealed to a life lived with God. Ellen G. White affirms that record: “The dying ruler had served his God faithfully, and had strengthened the confidence of the people in Jehovah as their Supreme Ruler.” Prophets and Kings, p. 340 · PK 340.3

This was not religious performance purchasing years of life; it was a servant appealing to a real relationship. And then the king wept. He was not ashamed to be weak before God, and neither tears nor calm is the measure of a genuine prayer—honesty is.

We do not need to pretend to be strong before bringing our weakness to God.

Now watch heaven answer. “But before Isaiah had left the middle courtyard, this message came to him from the Lord.” 2 Kings 20:4 (NLT) The God of David said: “I have heard your prayer and seen your tears. I will heal you, and three days from now you will get out of bed and go to the Temple of the Lord. I will add fifteen years to your life, and I will rescue you and this city from the king of Assyria. I will defend this city for my own honor and for the sake of my servant David.” 2 Kings 20:5–6 (NLT)

The reply was personal, and larger than one man: it spoke of Jerusalem and of David. Ellen G. White sums it up: “He whose ‘compassions fail not,’ heard the prayer of His servant. Lamentations 3:22.” Prophets and Kings, p. 341 · PK 341.3 Then came the means: “Then Isaiah said, ‘Make an ointment from figs.’ So Hezekiah’s servants spread the ointment over the boil, and Hezekiah recovered!” 2 Kings 20:7 (NLT) Prayer and practical care were not rivals. Ellen G. White tells us: “It is not a denial of faith to use such remedies as God has provided to alleviate pain and to aid nature in her work of restoration.” The Ministry of Healing, p. 231 · MH 231.3

A sign followed—at Hezekiah’s request the shadow on the sundial of Ahaz moved ten steps backward (2 Kings 20:8–11)—but the sign confirmed the promise; it was not the source of the healing. This was God’s particular promise to Hezekiah. It does not mean that everyone who prays will recover, or that every answer will come before the prophet reaches the courtyard. Ellen G. White is careful here: “if persons are not raised to health, they should not on this account be judged as wanting in faith.” The Ministry of Healing, p. 230 · MH 230.3

What the story gives every believer is this: God hears, God sees, and He is not indifferent to a servant in distress.

God’s response to Hezekiah was personal: his suffering had not escaped God’s attention.

Why may we come as Hezekiah came? Not because of a wall, or tears, or a record of service, but because of Jesus. “This High Priest of ours understands our weaknesses, for he faced all of the same testings we do, yet he did not sin. So let us come boldly to the throne of our gracious God. There we will receive his mercy, and we will find grace to help us when we need it most.” Hebrews 4:15–16 (NLT) Hezekiah received more years of earthly life.

In Christ our hope reaches beyond the number of our years: “I am the resurrection and the life. Anyone who believes in me will live, even after dying.” John 11:25 (NLT) And when God answers, the story is not over. Hezekiah rose from his bed to sing—“Only the living can praise you as I do today. Each generation tells of your faithfulness to the next” Isaiah 38:19 (NLT)—and Ellen G. White tells us he “vowed to spend his remaining days in willing service to the King of kings.” Prophets and Kings, p. 342 · PK 342.5

Later, when envoys from Babylon came to ask about his recovery, he showed them his treasures instead of telling them of his God, “and he became proud”; then he “humbled himself and repented of his pride.” 2 Chronicles 32:25–26 (NLT) Mercy asks a searching question: will I draw attention to the Giver or only to what I have gained? So name one fear honestly before God, ask a trusted person to pray with you, take one responsible step of care, and rest in the God who heard a dying king and saw his tears.

Bring God your fears and tears; entrust Him with the outcome—and let the mercy you receive shape the life you live.

Key Takeaways

  1. A crown could not remove Hezekiah’s vulnerability. “Set your affairs in order, for you are going to die. You will not recover from this illness.” 2 Kings 20:1 (NLT) Ellen White tells us “his case was beyond the power of man to help” Prophets and Kings, p. 340 · PK 340.1—and faithful service does not make anyone immune to a crisis.
  2. We do not need to pretend to be strong before bringing our weakness to God. “he turned his face to the wall and prayed to the Lord … Then he broke down and wept bitterly.” 2 Kings 20:2–3 (NLT) Ellen White says the outlook seemed utterly dark, “yet the king could still pray” Prophets and Kings, p. 340 · PK 340.2. The power was not in the wall; the prayer was directed to the Lord.
  3. God’s response to Hezekiah was personal: his suffering had not escaped God’s attention. “I have heard your prayer and seen your tears.” 2 Kings 20:5 (NLT) Ellen White writes, “He whose ‘compassions fail not,’ heard the prayer of His servant” Prophets and Kings, p. 341 · PK 341.3. It was a particular promise to Hezekiah, not a guarantee for every case; those who are not healed “should not on this account be judged as wanting in faith” The Ministry of Healing, p. 230 · MH 230.3.
  4. Bring God your fears and tears; entrust Him with the outcome. “So let us come boldly to the throne of our gracious God. There we will receive his mercy, and we will find grace to help us when we need it most.” Hebrews 4:16 (NLT) Then let the mercy you receive shape the life you live, drawing attention to the Giver rather than to what you have gained (2 Chronicles 32:25–26).
