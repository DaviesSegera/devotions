I Have No One

John 5 · 12 min read

By Dr. Davies Rene Segera

Jesus meets a lonely man at Bethesda in John 5. Reflect on His compassion and the invitation to respond when hope feels distant.

Hope & encouragementWaiting on GodForgiveness & grace
Watch the Short

Key Verse
“When Jesus saw him and knew he had been ill for a long time, he asked him, ‘Would you like to get well?’”
John 5:6 (NLT)

Jerusalem had a place for people like him. “Inside the city, near the Sheep Gate, was the pool of Bethesda, with five covered porches. Crowds of sick people—blind, lame, or paralyzed—lay on the porches” John 5:2-3 (NLT). Picture it honestly: not a hospital but a waiting room with no doctor, five colonnades of the city’s most hopeless, all watching one surface of water. The name is usually taken to mean house of mercy, and for most of the people lying there it had become a house of waiting.

The crowd believed that when the water stirred, the first one in would be healed — a belief John reports without endorsing it, and which some manuscripts spell out in a verse 4 that the NLT places in a footnote. Ellen White describes what that belief did to the crowd: “At certain seasons the waters of this pool were agitated, and it was commonly believed that this was the result of supernatural power”; and when it happened, “they rushed forward, trampling underfoot men, women, and children, weaker than themselves. Many could not get near the pool.” The Desire of Ages, p. 201 · DA 201.2.

Then John narrows the lens to one mat. “One of the men lying there had been sick for thirty-eight years” John 5:5 (NLT). Thirty-eight years. Longer than many of the people stepping over him had been alive. Ellen White puts his condition into a single sentence, and it is the whole reason this devotion exists: “Alone and friendless, feeling that he was shut out from God’s mercy, the sufferer had passed long years of misery.” The Desire of Ages, p. 202 · DA 202.1.

He was not only sick. He was sick in the house of mercy, convinced that mercy was for other people.

Now watch who moves first. “Afterward Jesus returned to Jerusalem for one of the Jewish holy days” John 5:1 (NLT). The city was full; the Temple courts were the place to be seen. Jesus went to the porches. Ellen White pictures it: “Walking alone, in apparent meditation and prayer, He came to the pool” The Desire of Ages, p. 201 · DA 201.3 — and among all that suffering, “the Saviour saw one case of supreme wretchedness.” The Desire of Ages, p. 202 · DA 202.1.

Then the verse chosen as the key to everything: “When Jesus saw him and knew he had been ill for a long time, he asked him, ‘Would you like to get well?’” John 5:6 (NLT). Read the order of it. Jesus saw. Jesus knew — not just that he was ill, but how long. And only then did Jesus speak. The man did not call out; he did not know who this was (verse 13 will tell us he could not have picked Jesus out of a crowd); he could not have crawled to Him if he had wanted to.

Every movement in this verse is from Jesus toward the man. And the knowing matters as much as the seeing. What the lonely fear most is not that God cannot see them but that He has not noticed how long it has been. Here it is written down: He knew.

Why ask a paralysed man whether he wants to be well? Because a question is a door, and Jesus was giving him a door to walk through with his own will. Ellen White: “A tender, compassionate face bent over him, and the words, ‘Wilt thou be made whole?’ arrested his attention.” “Hope came to his heart. He felt that in some way he was to have help.” But then: “the glow of encouragement soon faded. He remembered how often he had tried to reach the pool.” The Desire of Ages, p. 202 · DA 202.2.

Listen to how he answered: “‘I can’t, sir,’ the sick man said, ‘for I have no one to put me into the pool when the water bubbles up. Someone else always gets there ahead of me’” John 5:7 (NLT). He was asked whether he wanted to be well, and he answered with the reason it could not happen. Two sentences, and they hold the two oldest griefs of the human heart: I have no one, and someone else always gets there first.

Loneliness, and being overtaken. He had been on that mat so long that even a stranger’s kindness reached him as an accusation — as if the question were really, why haven’t you managed it yet? Thirty-eight years of disappointment will do that; it teaches you to hear every offer as a demand. And notice the one thing he did not see: he was staring at the pool and explaining its rules while the answer stood beside his mat.

“Jesus told him, ‘Stand up, pick up your mat, and walk!’” John 5:8 (NLT). No one carried him to the water. The water is never mentioned again. The man had said, I have no one to put me in, and Jesus did not answer by providing someone; He answered by making the pool unnecessary. That is worth sitting with, because we often pray for the helper when God intends to give the healing. Ellen White notices what Jesus did not do: “Jesus does not ask this sufferer to exercise faith in Him. He simply says, ‘Rise, take up thy bed, and walk.’” And what the man did: “Without question he sets his will to obey the command of Christ” — “every nerve and muscle thrills with new life, and healthful action comes to his crippled limbs.” The Desire of Ages, p. 202 · DA 202.3 “Instantly, the man was healed! He rolled up his sleeping mat and began walking!” John 5:9 (NLT).

In Steps to Christ she slows the moment down so we can see the hinge of it:

“The sick man might have said, ‘Lord, if Thou wilt make me whole, I will obey Thy word.’ But, no, he believed Christ’s word, believed that he was made whole, and he made the effort at once; he willed to walk, and he did walk. He acted on the word of Christ, and God gave the power. He was made whole.”. Steps to Christ, p. 50 · SC 50.1

He had no evidence but a voice. Thirty-eight years of evidence said the opposite. He moved anyway — and the strength came in the moving. “The man might have stopped to doubt, and lost his one chance of healing. But he believed Christ’s word, and in acting upon it he received strength.” The Desire of Ages, p. 203 · DA 203.1

This is where Ellen White takes the story, and it is where Scripture takes it too: the deepest paralysis is not in the legs. “Through the same faith we may receive spiritual healing. By sin we have been severed from the life of God. Our souls are palsied.” The Desire of Ages, p. 203 · DA 203.2. We lie on our own porches — the habit we have stopped fighting, the bitterness we have made peace with, the prayer life that has been flat for so long that we no longer expect it to move — and we explain to God why nothing can change: no one to help, everyone else ahead.

And the word that comes is the same word: stand up.

“You are a sinner. You cannot atone for your past sins; you cannot change your heart and make yourself holy. But God promises to do all this for you through Christ. You believe that promise. You confess your sins and give yourself to God. You will to serve Him. Just as surely as you do this, God will fulfill His word to you.”. Steps to Christ, p. 51 · SC 51.1

Then the line that has steadied more people than can be counted: “Do not wait to feel that you are made whole, but say, ‘I believe it; it is so, not because I feel it, but because God has promised.’” Steps to Christ, p. 51 · SC 51.2 “Put your will on the side of Christ. Will to serve Him, and in acting upon His word you will receive strength.” The Desire of Ages, p. 203 · DA 203.2. Forgiveness works like the man’s legs: it is received in the act of trusting, not in waiting for a feeling to arrive first.

Let me be exact here, because it matters. This counsel is about the forgiveness of sin and the healing of the heart; it is not a formula for the body. Scripture never turns one man’s healing into a guarantee for every mat — Paul asked three times and heard, “My grace is all you need. My power works best in weakness” 2 Corinthians 12:9 (NLT). Believing God does not mean refusing the doctor or stopping the medicine He has provided. It means you are no longer alone on the porch.

There is one more thing Jesus did for this man, and it is easy to miss. The healing happened on the Sabbath, the leaders objected to the mat, and when they demanded a name, “The man didn’t know, for Jesus had disappeared into the crowd” John 5:13 (NLT). Ellen White: “he looked around for his Deliverer; but Jesus was lost in the crowd,” and “the man feared that he would not know Him if he should see Him again.” The Desire of Ages, p. 203 · DA 203.3.

Healed, and alone again. But then: “But afterward Jesus found him in the Temple” John 5:14 (NLT). Found him. The man who had no one was sought out twice by the same Person — once on the porch, once among the worshippers. That is the pattern of grace: it does not heal you and leave you to work out who did it. What Jesus said there was a mercy dressed as a warning: “Now you are well; so stop sinning, or something even worse may happen to you” John 5:14 (NLT).

In this man’s case there was a link between his sin and his suffering — Ellen White says plainly that “his disease was in a great degree the result of his own sin” The Desire of Ages, p. 202 · DA 202.1 — and Jesus, who knew the whole thirty-eight years, addressed it. But be careful what you build on that. The same Jesus, asked whose sin had caused a man’s blindness, answered, “It was not because of his sins or his parents’ sins” John 9:3 (NLT).

Do not read anyone’s illness, including your own, as a verdict. What Jesus is saying is that there is something worse than thirty-eight years on a mat, and that it is possible to get your legs back and still be lost. He wanted the man whole, not just walking.

So let me speak to whoever said the title of this devotion under their breath this morning. I have no one. You may be surrounded by people and still mean it. The Bible does not scold you for the feeling; it wrote your sentence down first. “I look for someone to come and help me, but no one gives me a passing thought! No one will help me; no one cares a bit what happens to me” Psalm 142:4 (NLT) — that is David, in a cave, in the Bible.

And the same book answers: “Even if my father and mother abandon me, the LORD will hold me close” Psalm 27:10 (NLT); “The LORD is close to the brokenhearted; he rescues those whose spirits are crushed” Psalm 34:18 (NLT); “I will never fail you. I will never abandon you” Hebrews 13:5 (NLT). Bethesda is what those promises look like with a face. He came to the porch on purpose, He picked out the one who had waited longest, and He came back for him afterwards.

Two honest things follow. First, tell Him the truth the way the man did — the whole thirty-eight years of it — and then listen for the question underneath your explanation: would you like to get well? Answer the question, not the obstacle. Second, do not stay silent with people either. In Mark’s Gospel, “Four men arrived carrying a paralyzed man on a mat” Mark 2:3 (NLT) — God’s usual way of answering I have no one is to send someone, and His people are meant to be the carriers.

Say the words out loud to a pastor, a friend, a doctor: I have no one to put me in. And if the weight you are carrying today has made it hard to keep going at all, please tell someone you trust today. That is not weakness; it is the first step off the mat.

He is still walking the porches. Not only the Temple courts where the successful gather, but the colonnades where people have stopped expecting anything. He sees you. He knows how long. And He is not waiting for the water to move or for someone to arrive; He is asking the question, and the answer is not a reason. Ellen White: “Do not wait to feel that you are made whole. Believe His word, and it will be fulfilled.” The Desire of Ages, p. 203 · DA 203.2.

What is He telling you to stand up from today? You may have no one — but you have never been unseen.

Key Takeaways

  1. “I have no one” is a real grief, and Scripture writes it down before it answers it — thirty-eight years, five porches, and someone else always first. God does not scold the feeling; David said it too.
  2. Every movement in the key verse is from Jesus toward the man — when Jesus saw him and knew he had been ill for a long time. He did not call out, did not know who Jesus was, and could not reach Him. Jesus came.
  3. He knew how long — what the lonely fear is not that God cannot see them but that He has not noticed the years. He had.
  4. The man answered an offer with an explanation — long disappointment teaches us to hear even grace as a demand. Answer the question, not the obstacle.
  5. Jesus never used the pool — to I have no one to put me in He did not send a helper; He made the pool unnecessary. Do not pray only for the carrier when God intends the healing.
  6. The strength came in the moving — he willed to walk, and he did walk; he acted on the word of Christ, and God gave the power. Do not wait to feel that you are made whole. This is counsel for forgiveness and the heart, never a reason to refuse the doctor.
  7. He was found twice — on the porch and in the Temple. Grace does not heal you and leave you to work out who did it; and stop sinning was a mercy, because it is possible to walk and still be lost. No one’s illness is a verdict.
