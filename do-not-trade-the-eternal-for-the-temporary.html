Today's Devotion

Do Not Trade the Eternal for the Temporary

Genesis 25 · 7 min read
Key Verse
“Then Jacob gave Esau some bread and lentil stew. Esau ate the meal, then got up and left. He showed contempt for his rights as the firstborn.”
Genesis 25:34 (NLT)
“Make sure that no one is immoral or godless like Esau, who traded his birthright as the firstborn son for a single meal.”
Hebrews 12:16 (NLT)

How much can one meal cost? For Esau, the price was a birthright. Genesis introduces the twin sons of Isaac and Rebekah with a word from the Lord before their birth—“your older son will serve your younger son” Genesis 25:23 (NLT)—and then a contrast: “Esau became a skillful hunter. He was an outdoorsman, but Jacob had a quiet temperament, preferring to stay at home.” Genesis 25:27 (NLT) Esau was the firstborn (Genesis 25:25), and the rights of the firstborn were his. Genesis does not spell out everything those rights included, but the promises God gave to Abraham and confirmed to Isaac ran through this family (Genesis 26:3–4), and later Scripture shows the firstborn’s rights carrying both inheritance and leadership (Deuteronomy 21:17; 1 Chronicles 5:1–2).

Ellen G. White helps us see why the birthright mattered so much in Isaac’s household:

“They were taught to regard the birthright as a matter of great importance, for it included not only an inheritance of worldly wealth but spiritual pre-eminence. He who received it was to be the priest of his family, and in the line of his posterity the Redeemer of the world would come.” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 177 · PP 177.3

Yet, she observes, Esau “had no love for devotion, no inclination to a religious life” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 178 · PP 178.1.

We can possess a privilege and still fail to appreciate its worth.

“One day when Jacob was cooking some stew, Esau arrived home from the wilderness exhausted and hungry.” Genesis 25:29 (NLT) His hunger was real, and Scripture does not mock it. “I’m starved! Give me some of that red stew!” Genesis 25:30 (NLT) Jacob turned a meal into a transaction: “All right,” Jacob replied, “but trade me your rights as the firstborn son.” Genesis 25:31 (NLT) And Esau measured his inheritance by the hunger of the moment: “Look, I’m dying of starvation!” said Esau. “What good is my birthright to me now?” Genesis 25:32 (NLT)

Hunger did not change the birthright’s value; it changed Esau’s perception of its value. Ellen G. White describes the same heart:

“A short time at most would have secured him food in his father’s tents, but to satisfy the desire of the moment he carelessly bartered the glorious heritage that God Himself had promised to his fathers. His whole interest was in the present. He was ready to sacrifice the heavenly to the earthly, to exchange a future good for a momentary indulgence.” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 179 · PP 179.1

Pressure can explain why a decision feels difficult without making every decision under pressure wise.

Never let a temporary condition determine the value of an eternal possession.

Jacob required an oath: “But Jacob said, ‘First you must swear that your birthright is mine.’ So Esau swore an oath, thereby selling all his rights as the firstborn to his brother, Jacob.” Genesis 25:33 (NLT) Only then came the food: “Then Jacob gave Esau some bread and lentil stew. Esau ate the meal, then got up and left. He showed contempt for his rights as the firstborn.” Genesis 25:34 (NLT) Three plain verbs—ate, got up, left—and then the verdict. The stew was not evil and the hunger was not sin; the tragedy was the value Esau placed on what God had entrusted to him.

Hebrews reads the scene the same way: “godless like Esau, who traded his birthright as the firstborn son for a single meal.” Hebrews 12:16 (NLT) Nor is Jacob the hero here. Scripture records that he set a price on his brother’s hunger, and Ellen G. White says plainly that Jacob “seized upon his advantage, and offered to satisfy his brother’s hunger at the price of the birthright” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 179 · PP 179.1. Jacob valued the right thing but pursued it in the wrong way; Esau possessed the right thing but valued it too little. One brother’s wrongdoing does not erase the other’s responsibility.

The meal solved a temporary problem; the exchange reached far beyond the meal.

Hebrews continues: “afterward, when he wanted his father’s blessing, he was rejected. It was too late for repentance, even though he begged with bitter tears.” Hebrews 12:17 (NLT) That points to the later scene in Genesis 27, when the blessing had already been given and Isaac declared, “that blessing must stand!” Genesis 27:33 (NLT) Esau wept for a lost blessing, not for his sin, and Ellen G. White reads the verse with care:

“Esau was not shut out from the privilege of seeking God’s favor by repentance, but he could find no means of recovering the birthright. His grief did not spring from conviction of sin; he did not desire to be reconciled to God.” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 181 · PP 181.2

Some earthly consequences cannot be reversed; God’s mercy toward the one who truly repents is never withdrawn. So the question comes to us: What temporary thing am I allowing to become more valuable than something God has entrusted to me? Integrity for advantage, purity for pleasure, Sabbath rest for gain, prayer for distraction, conviction for approval—the exchanges are not new, only the prices. Ellen G. White applies Esau’s story to the gospel itself: he “represents those who lightly value the redemption purchased for them by Christ, and are ready to sacrifice their heirship to heaven for the perishable things of earth” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 181 · PP 181.3.

The answer is not stronger willpower but a greater treasure. Jesus, hungry after forty days in the wilderness, was invited to let appetite decide—and He answered, “People do not live by bread alone, but by every word that comes from the mouth of God.” Matthew 4:4 (NLT) Esau allowed present hunger to decide what mattered most; Jesus, when hungry, remained submitted to His Father’s Word. And He offers an inheritance greater than anything Esau held: “a priceless inheritance—an inheritance that is kept in heaven for you, pure and undefiled, beyond the reach of change and decay.” 1 Peter 1:4 (NLT)

If temporary things have taken too high a place in your life, the answer is not despair. Come to Christ; let Him reorder your desires and strengthen your self-control by His Spirit.

Make Christ your treasure, and do not trade the eternal for the temporary.
