Today's Devotion

Courage in the Storm

Acts 27 · 6 min read
Key Verse
“So take courage! For I believe God. It will be just as he said.”
Acts 27:25 (NLT)

What can hold us steady when we cannot see how the journey will end? Paul was travelling to Rome as a prisoner, in the custody of a Roman officer, Julius. The sailing season was closing when the ship reached Fair Havens on Crete, and he warned the officers: “Men,” he said, “I believe there is trouble ahead if we go on—shipwreck, loss of cargo, and danger to our lives as well.” Acts 27:10 (NLT) The officer listened to the captain and the owner instead (Acts 27:11–12). A gentle south wind made that decision look right—until “a wind of typhoon strength ... burst across the island and blew us out to sea.” Acts 27:14 (NLT, excerpt)

The crew did what experienced sailors do—braced the hull with ropes, threw the cargo and then the ship’s gear overboard (Acts 27:16–19). Effort and skill were not the problem; the sky was. “The terrible storm raged for many days, blotting out the sun and the stars, until at last all hope was gone.” Acts 27:20 (NLT) Ellen G. White writes, “For fourteen days they drifted under a sunless and starless heaven.” The Acts of the Apostles, p. 442 · AA 442.2 That is not a failure of faith; it is what happens when people have worked to exhaustion and still cannot see.

Hope can fail in us; the God we belong to does not fail.

Then a prisoner stood up on the deck. No one had eaten for a long time (Acts 27:21). Paul began where honesty required—they should have listened at Crete—and then gave them what he had been given: “But take courage! None of you will lose your lives, even though the ship will go down. For last night an angel of the God to whom I belong and whom I serve stood beside me, and he said, ‘Don’t be afraid, Paul, for you will surely stand trial before Caesar! What’s more, God in his goodness has granted safety to everyone sailing with you.’” Acts 27:22–24 (NLT)

Notice what that promise did and did not say. Not that the storm would stop, or the ship be saved: the people would live, and Paul would stand before Caesar (Acts 23:11). On that word Paul staked everything: “So take courage! For I believe God. It will be just as he said.” Acts 27:25 (NLT) He added that they would be shipwrecked on an island (Acts 27:26). Ellen G. White fills in those hours for him:

“He grasped by faith the arm of Infinite Power, and his heart was stayed upon God.... As he earnestly pleaded with God to spare their lives, it was revealed to him that his prayer was granted.” The Acts of the Apostles, p. 442 · AA 442.2

Faith can hold to God’s word while the way ahead remains unclear.

Ellen G. White writes, “At these words, hope revived. Passengers and crew roused from their apathy.” The Acts of the Apostles, p. 443 · AA 443.2 Trust did not end the work; it started it again. Nearing land the crew threw out four anchors and “prayed for daylight” Acts 27:29 (NLT, excerpt). When some sailors tried to slip away in the lifeboat, Paul told the officer and the soldiers, “You will all die unless the sailors stay aboard.” Acts 27:31 (NLT) The soldiers cut the ropes and let the boat go. The promise had not made seamanship unnecessary: the people who knew the ship were needed at their posts.

Then comes the chapter’s most ordinary instruction: as day dawned, Paul urged them all to eat, promising that not one of them would be harmed (Acts 27:33–34). “Then he took some bread, gave thanks to God before them all, and broke off a piece and ate it. Then everyone was encouraged and began to eat—all 276 of us who were on board.” Acts 27:35–37 (NLT) He gave thanks before the shore was in sight. Ellen G. White describes a worn and discouraged company “who but for Paul would have become desperate, joined with the apostle in partaking of food.” The Acts of the Apostles, p. 444 · AA 444.2

Paul was in the same danger as everyone else, and not the only one who acted: the sailors’ skill, the soldiers’ obedience and Julius’s authority all belong to this rescue. His encouragement was not advice from safety; it was truth, bread and a steady presence from someone frightened too.

Hope can be heard in our words and felt in our care.

Morning showed a bay with a beach; they ran for it, struck a shoal, and the ship began to break apart (Acts 27:39–41). The soldiers wanted to kill the prisoners so that none could escape, but Julius stopped them to spare Paul; some swam, the rest came in on planks and broken pieces of the ship, and “everyone escaped safely to shore.” Acts 27:42–44 (NLT, excerpt) Ellen G. White adds, “When the roll was called, not one was missing.” The Acts of the Apostles, p. 445 · AA 445.1 The ship was lost, the cargo was lost, and everyone was alive, exactly as God had said.

On Malta the islanders lit a fire in the cold rain and welcomed them. Paul, gathering sticks for it, was bitten by a snake driven out by the heat and was unharmed; he prayed for the sick father of Publius, who was healed, and others were healed too (Acts 28:1–10). Paul received help and gave it.

That promise was made to that ship’s company on that voyage; it is not a guarantee that every believer will survive every danger or recover every loss. What God has promised us is larger and surer: Christ Jesus “died for us and was raised to life for us, and he is sitting in the place of honor at God’s right hand, pleading for us.” Romans 8:34 (NLT, excerpt) Nothing in all creation can separate us from that love—not trouble, not danger, not death itself (Romans 8:35–39). So we may come to Him now: “There we will receive his mercy, and we will find grace to help us when we need it most.” Hebrews 4:16 (NLT, excerpt)

Bring Him the fear you actually have. Ask for the help you need, and let someone give it. Then look around the boat: someone near you has not eaten. How can I trust God’s word and share His care in the storm I face today?

Bring Christ your fear, trust what He has promised, and carry hope to the person beside you.

Key Takeaways

  1. Hope can fail in us; the God we belong to does not fail. “The terrible storm raged for many days, blotting out the sun and the stars, until at last all hope was gone.” Acts 27:20 (NLT) Ellen White tells us they drifted “under a sunless and starless heaven” The Acts of the Apostles, p. 442 · AA 442.2—exhaustion and lost hope are in this account, and they are not treated as unbelief.
  2. Faith can hold to God’s word while the way ahead remains unclear. “So take courage! For I believe God. It will be just as he said.” Acts 27:25 (NLT) Ellen White writes that Paul “grasped by faith the arm of Infinite Power, and his heart was stayed upon God” The Acts of the Apostles, p. 442 · AA 442.2—and the promise he trusted included the loss of the ship.
  3. Hope can be heard in our words and felt in our care. “Then he took some bread, gave thanks to God before them all, and broke off a piece and ate it.” Acts 27:35 (NLT) Ellen White says that at his words “hope revived. Passengers and crew roused from their apathy” The Acts of the Apostles, p. 443 · AA 443.2—trust put them back to work and back to the table.
  4. Bring Christ your fear, trust what He has promised, and carry hope to the person beside you. “There we will receive his mercy, and we will find grace to help us when we need it most.” Hebrews 4:16 (NLT, excerpt) The rescue of 276 people was God keeping a particular promise; our lasting assurance is Christ, who “died for us and was raised to life for us ... pleading for us.” Romans 8:34 (NLT, excerpt)

Sources and Further ReadingScripture: Acts 27:1–44, with the central scene in 27:20–38 and the key text in 27:25; Acts 28:1–10 as the brief aftermath; Acts 23:11 and 25:10–12 as background; Romans 8:31–39 and Hebrews 4:14–16 (NLT). Ellen G. White: The Acts of the Apostles, chapter 42, “The Voyage and Shipwreck,” pp. 439–446 (AA 442.2, 443.2, 444.2, 445.1) — her account of the voyage, of Paul’s faith and his prayer for the ship’s company, of the encouragement to eat, and of the rescue and the stay on Malta. Her earlier account of the same voyage, Sketches from the Life of Paul, chapter 24, “The Voyage and Shipwreck,” pp. 261–271, was read alongside it.
