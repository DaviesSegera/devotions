Bring Mark With You

Acts 15:36–41; 2 Timothy 4:11 · 10 min read

By Dr. Davies Rene Segera · 2026-11-25

John Mark turns back from a mission, and Paul and Barnabas disagree over taking him again. Years later, Paul asks for him by name. Reflect on how God works through failure, encouragement, and reconciliation.

Hope & encouragementForgiveness & graceLove & service

Key Verse
“Only Luke is with me. Bring Mark with you when you come, for he will be helpful to me in my ministry.”
2 Timothy 4:11 (NLT)

Have you ever been remembered for your worst moment? Perhaps you began something with real hope, only to step back when it grew difficult. The story of John Mark is for anyone who has wondered whether one failure has closed the door for good.

Mark grew up close to the early church. When Peter was set free from prison, Luke tells us where he went: “When he realized this, he went to the home of Mary, the mother of John Mark, where many were gathered for prayer.” Acts 12:12, NLT Ellen G. White describes that household: “Mark’s mother was a convert to the Christian religion, and her home at Jerusalem was an asylum for the disciples.” The Acts of the Apostles, p. 166 · AA 166.3, excerpt Here, an asylum means a place of refuge. Mark saw faith lived out in his own home.

In time, Mark joined Barnabas and Saul in their work. “When Barnabas and Saul had finished their mission to Jerusalem, they returned, taking John Mark with them.” Acts 12:25, NLT When the two missionaries set out for Cyprus, Luke adds, “John Mark went with them as their assistant.” Acts 13:5, NLT, excerpt Ellen White describes his early desire to serve. “He felt the favor of God in his heart and longed to devote himself entirely to the work of the gospel ministry.” The Acts of the Apostles, p. 166 · AA 166.3, excerpt

But the road was harder than he expected. “Paul and his companions then left Paphos by ship for Pamphylia, landing at the port town of Perga. There John Mark left them and returned to Jerusalem.” Acts 13:13, NLT Ellen White describes the fear Mark faced at Perga.

“It was here that Mark, overwhelmed with fear and discouragement, wavered for a time in his purpose to give himself wholeheartedly to the Lord’s work. Unused to hardships, he was disheartened by the perils and privations of the way.”The Acts of the Apostles, p. 169 · AA 169.3, excerpt

Many of us know that feeling. We begin eagerly, then the cost becomes real, and fear grows louder than faith. Mark’s story could have ended at Perga.

Mark turned back at Perga, but God had not given up on him.

Some time later, Paul suggested a return visit to the young churches. “After some time Paul said to Barnabas, ‘Let’s go back and visit each city where we previously preached the word of the Lord, to see how the new believers are doing.’” Acts 15:36, NLT

Would Mark go with them again? “Barnabas agreed and wanted to take along John Mark.” Acts 15:37, NLT “But Paul disagreed strongly, since John Mark had deserted them in Pamphylia and had not continued with them in their work.” Acts 15:38, NLT “Their disagreement was so sharp that they separated. Barnabas took John Mark with him and sailed for Cyprus.” Acts 15:39, NLT

Paul wanted to know he could count on Mark when the work grew hard. Ellen White explains his concern.

“He was not inclined to excuse Mark’s weakness in deserting the work for the safety and comforts of home. He urged that one with so little stamina was unfitted for a work requiring patience, self-denial, bravery, devotion, faith, and a willingness to sacrifice, if need be, even life itself.”The Acts of the Apostles, p. 202 · AA 202.1, excerpt

Barnabas saw the same young man differently. Ellen White puts the two views side by side: “This desertion caused Paul to judge Mark unfavorably, and even severely, for a time. Barnabas, on the other hand, was inclined to excuse him because of his inexperience.” The Acts of the Apostles, p. 170 · AA 170.1, excerpt

Both men loved God and cared about the believers, so we should be slow to take sides. Even after they went their separate ways, God continued to care for the churches. “Paul chose Silas, and as he left, the believers entrusted him to the Lord’s gracious care.” Acts 15:40, NLT “Then he traveled throughout Syria and Cilicia, strengthening the churches there.” Acts 15:41, NLT While Paul and Silas visited the churches, Mark had another opportunity to serve alongside Barnabas.

Two faithful men disagreed sharply, yet God kept caring for His churches and for Mark.

Barnabas was the right man to stand beside Mark. Luke introduces him as “Joseph, the one the apostles nicknamed Barnabas (which means ‘Son of Encouragement’).” Acts 4:36, NLT, excerpt He had lived up to that name before. When the believers in Jerusalem were afraid of the newly converted Saul, “Barnabas brought him to the apostles and told them how Saul had seen the Lord on the way to Damascus and how the Lord had spoken to Saul.” Acts 9:27, NLT, excerpt When others were afraid of Saul, Barnabas stood beside him and spoke on his behalf.

Now it was Mark who needed someone to stand beside him. Ellen White describes what he saw: “He felt anxious that Mark should not abandon the ministry, for he saw in him qualifications that would fit him to be a useful worker for Christ.” The Acts of the Apostles, p. 170 · AA 170.1, excerpt Barnabas knew what had happened at Perga. Even so, he took Mark with him and stayed beside him as he grew.

In the way Barnabas looked at Mark, we catch a glimpse of how Jesus looks at us. Writing about Christ’s ministry, Ellen White says, “Looking upon them with hope, He inspired hope. Meeting them with confidence, He inspired trust.” Education, p. 80 · Ed 80.1, excerpt Jesus does not ignore our failures, but He sees what His grace can still do in us. Paul himself later wrote, “And I am certain that God, who began the good work within you, will continue his work until it is finally finished on the day when Christ Jesus returns.” Philippians 1:6, NLT

Sometimes encouragement means staying beside someone as God helps them grow.

Years passed. In his letters, Paul now speaks of Mark with warmth. Writing from prison, Paul says, “Aristarchus, who is in prison with me, sends you his greetings, and so does Mark, Barnabas’s cousin. As you were instructed before, make Mark welcome if he comes your way.” Colossians 4:10, NLT Speaking of Mark and two other Jewish believers, he adds, “they are working with me here for the Kingdom of God. And what a comfort they have been!” Colossians 4:11, NLT, excerpt

Ellen White sums up the change: “Paul was afterward reconciled to Mark and received him as a fellow laborer.” The Acts of the Apostles, p. 170 · AA 170.2, excerpt She also tells us how Mark grew: “Under the blessing of God, and the wise training of Barnabas, he developed into a valuable worker.” The Acts of the Apostles, p. 170 · AA 170.1, excerpt

The most moving words come near the end of Paul’s life. In a letter to Timothy, Paul speaks of being left behind. “Demas has deserted me because he loves the things of this life and has gone to Thessalonica.” 2 Timothy 4:10, NLT, excerpt Then he adds, “Only Luke is with me. Bring Mark with you when you come, for he will be helpful to me in my ministry.” 2 Timothy 4:11, NLT

Years before, the word “deserted” had been used about Mark. Now it describes someone else, and Mark is the friend Paul wants beside him. Reconciliation asked something of both men. Mark kept serving, and Paul welcomed the man he had once been unwilling to take with him. That is the spirit Paul urged on every believer: “Instead, be kind to each other, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, just as God through Christ has forgiven you.” Ephesians 4:32, NLT

The young man who once turned back became the friend Paul asked for at the end.

Mark also knew Peter, a disciple who understood what it meant to fail. On the night of His arrest, Jesus had told Peter, “But I have pleaded in prayer for you, Simon, that your faith should not fail. So when you have repented and turned to me again, strengthen your brothers.” Luke 22:32, NLT Years later, Peter wrote, “Your sister church here in Babylon sends you greetings, and so does my son Mark.” 1 Peter 5:13, NLT Christians have long understood this to be the same Mark. An early Christian tradition, preserved by the church historian Eusebius, remembers him as Peter’s interpreter, who wrote down what Peter taught about Jesus. The Lord who restored Peter also restored Mark.

Where do you see yourself in this story? If you are like Mark, Jesus has not written you off. Bring your failure to Him and take the next faithful step He shows you. Perhaps, like Paul, you know what it is to be let down. You can take the hurt seriously and still leave room for God to change someone. Trust can take time to grow again. And if God is calling you to be a Barnabas, look for someone who has stepped away from faith or service. A visit, a prayer, or an invitation to serve with you may help them begin again.

With Jesus, a failure does not have to be the last word in our story.

Reflect

  1. Is there a past failure that still makes me feel God cannot use me? What would it mean to bring it to Jesus today?
  2. When someone has let me down, how can I be wise and still leave room for God to change them?
  3. Who comes to mind when I think of someone who has stepped back from faith or service? How could I encourage them this week?

Key Takeaways

  1. A failure is not the end of the story. Mark turned back at Perga, yet God kept working in his life. Acts 13:13 · quoted above
  2. Sincere believers can disagree and still be reconciled. Paul and Barnabas parted ways over Mark. Years later, Paul welcomed Mark as a fellow worker. Acts 15:37–39 · quoted above
  3. God can make us useful again. At the end of his life, Paul asked for the very man he had once refused. 2 Timothy 4:11 · quoted above

A prayer for today

Heavenly Father, thank You that our failures do not have the final word when we place our lives in Your hands. Thank You for Jesus, who restored Peter and gave Mark a new beginning. Where fear has made us turn back, give us courage. Where others have let us down, give us wisdom and a forgiving heart. Help us to encourage others as Barnabas did. Open our eyes to what Your grace can do in the person beside us. Heal the divisions among Your people, and finish the good work You have begun in each of us. In Jesus’ name, Amen.

— Dr. Davies Rene Segera
