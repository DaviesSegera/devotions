Today's Devotion

When God Sends a Peacemaker

1 Samuel 25 · 7 min read
Key Verse
“Praise the Lord, the God of Israel, who has sent you to meet me today!”
1 Samuel 25:32 (NLT, excerpt)
“Thank God for your good sense! Bless you for keeping me from murder and from carrying out vengeance with my own hands.”
1 Samuel 25:33 (NLT)

Sometimes God’s answer to our anger comes through someone asking us to stop. In 1 Samuel 25, David—anointed but not yet king, a fugitive in Judah—sent ten young men to a wealthy landowner named Nabal at sheep-shearing time. His men had protected Nabal’s shepherds; the request was courteous: “Please share any provisions you might have on hand with us and with your friend David.” 1 Samuel 25:8 (NLT) Nabal’s answer was contempt: “Who is this fellow David?” Nabal sneered to the young men. “Who does this son of Jesse think he is?” 1 Samuel 25:10 (NLT, excerpt)

When the report reached David, his reply was short: “Get your swords!” 1 Samuel 25:13 (NLT, excerpt) Four hundred men set out with him, and he swore that not one male of Nabal’s household would be alive by morning (1 Samuel 25:21–22). The insult was real; the response was a plan to kill many people who had wronged no one. Ellen G. White describes the moment plainly: “This impulsive movement was more in harmony with the character of Saul than with that of David, but the son of Jesse had yet to learn of patience in the school of affliction.” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 665 · PP 665.2

An injury does not remove our responsibility for what we do next.

Meanwhile a servant went to Abigail, Nabal’s wife, told her what had happened, and said, “You need to know this and figure out what to do, for there is going to be trouble for our master and his whole family.” 1 Samuel 25:17 (NLT, excerpt) “Abigail wasted no time.” 1 Samuel 25:18 (NLT, excerpt) She loaded donkeys with provisions, sent her servants ahead, and rode out to meet an armed and angry man. She bowed low before David and spoke—and it was not flattery. She took the matter onto herself, offered the gift, and said the hard thing: the Lord had kept David “from murdering and taking vengeance into your own hands,” 1 Samuel 25:26 (NLT, excerpt) the Lord would give him a lasting dynasty, and when God made him leader of Israel he must not have to carry “the staggering burden of needless bloodshed and vengeance” 1 Samuel 25:31 (NLT, excerpt) on his conscience.

Respectful in manner, it was a correction in substance. Ellen G. White writes of her appeal:

“These words could have come only from the lips of one who had partaken of the wisdom from above. The piety of Abigail, like the fragrance of a flower, breathed out all unconsciously in face and word and action. The Spirit of the Son of God was abiding in her soul. Her speech, seasoned with grace, and full of kindness and peace, shed a heavenly influence.” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 667 · PP 667.1

A wise word can become an instrument of God’s restraining grace.

Wise concern becomes practical help, and peacemaking can require the courage to say that a course of action is wrong.

Then the story turns on what David did with her words. “Praise the Lord, the God of Israel, who has sent you to meet me today! Thank God for your good sense! Bless you for keeping me from murder and from carrying out vengeance with my own hands.” 1 Samuel 25:32–33 (NLT, excerpt) He credited God first, thanked the person who corrected him, named plainly what he had been about to do, accepted her gift, and changed his decision: “Return home in peace. I have heard what you said. We will not kill your husband.” 1 Samuel 25:35 (NLT, excerpt)

This was the David who had spared Saul (1 Samuel 24; 26); restraint once does not make anyone immune to anger the next time, and position, power and a real grievance put no one beyond correction. Ellen G. White writes:

“With a humble heart he received the rebuke.... He gave thanks and blessings because she advised him righteously. There are many who, when they are reproved, think it praiseworthy if they receive the rebuke without becoming impatient; but how few take reproof with gratitude of heart and bless those who seek to save them from pursuing an evil course.” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 667 · PP 667.3

James says it in a sentence: “Human anger does not produce the righteousness God desires.” James 1:20 (NLT) Abigail told Nabal the next morning, when he was sober; he collapsed, and about ten days later “the Lord struck him, and he died.” 1 Samuel 25:38 (NLT, excerpt) David later married her. But the story’s turning point is verse 35, not verse 38: harm was prevented because a courageous word was humbly received.

A teachable heart can change direction before anger becomes harm.

We may need Abigail’s courage or David’s humility—often both in the same week. Peacemaking is not keeping things quiet; a quiet room is not necessarily a just or safe room. It looks like refusing to repeat an inflammatory rumour, offering a calm and honest correction, or, where someone is in real danger, getting appropriate help rather than approaching the danger alone. And being corrected is not merely listening politely; it is stopping a wrong course once the truth is plain—testing counsel by Scripture and the facts, and adding apology and repair where harm has been done.

Neither task belongs to women as managers of men’s anger or to children as mediators of adult conflict; Ellen G. White’s wish reaches the whole church: “Would that there were many more like this woman of Israel, who would soothe the irritated feelings, prevent rash impulses, and quell great evils by words of calm and well-directed wisdom.” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 667 · PP 667.1 Neither task makes us saviours or guarantees another’s response; our hope rests elsewhere. “For Christ himself has brought peace to us,” Ephesians 2:14 (NLT, excerpt)

Paul writes—peace made in His own body on the cross, reconciling us to God. And Peter tells us that Jesus “did not retaliate when he was insulted, nor threaten revenge when he suffered. He left his case in the hands of God, who always judges fairly. He personally carried our sins in his body on the cross so that we can be dead to sin and live for what is right.” 1 Peter 2:23–24 (NLT, excerpt) David was kept from sin by a word he received; we are saved by the One who carried our sins. Am I willing both to speak wisely and to receive wise correction?

Receive Christ’s grace, speak truth with care, listen with humility, and surrender revenge to God.
