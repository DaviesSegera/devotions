The Right Cause, the Wrong Way: When Moses Ran Ahead of God

Exodus 2:11–25 · 10 min read

By Dr. Davies Rene Segera · 2026-11-03

Moses cared about his people's suffering, but his attempt to rescue them ended in flight to Midian. Reflect on God's timing, humility in the wilderness, and hope after failure through Christ.

Waiting on GodHumility & obedienceWisdom & discernmentForgiveness & graceFaith & trust

Key Verse
“God heard their groaning, and he remembered his covenant promise to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. He looked down on the people of Israel and knew it was time to act.”
Exodus 2:24–25 (NLT)

Have you ever been sure that your cause was right, only to discover that you had gone about it the wrong way? Moses knew what that was like. His heart was stirred by the suffering of his people, yet his first attempt to help them ended in death, rejection, and a long flight into the desert.

Moses had grown up in Pharaoh’s palace, but he never forgot where he came from. Hebrews describes his choice: “It was by faith that Moses, when he grew up, refused to be called the son of Pharaoh’s daughter.” Hebrews 11:24, NLT “He chose to share the oppression of God’s people instead of enjoying the fleeting pleasures of sin.” Hebrews 11:25, NLT Exodus shows that choice in action: “Many years later, when Moses had grown up, he went out to visit his own people, the Hebrews, and he saw how hard they were forced to work. During his visit, he saw an Egyptian beating one of his fellow Hebrews.” Exodus 2:11, NLT

What followed took only a moment: “After looking in all directions to make sure no one was watching, Moses killed the Egyptian and hid the body in the sand.” Exodus 2:12, NLT In the book of Acts, Stephen explains what Moses had been thinking: “Moses assumed his fellow Israelites would realize that God had sent him to rescue them, but they didn’t.” Acts 7:25, NLT

Ellen G. White describes what had been building in Moses’ heart for years:

“His thoughts often turned upon the abject condition of his people, and he visited his brethren in their servitude, and encouraged them with the assurance that God would work for their deliverance. Often, stung to resentment by the sight of injustice and oppression, he burned to avenge their wrongs.”Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 246 · PP 246.2, excerpt

Moses cared deeply, and God cared too. But anger, even in the face of real injustice, is a poor guide: “Human anger does not produce the righteousness God desires.” James 1:20, NLT

Ellen White names the deeper problem:

“In slaying the Egyptian, Moses had fallen into the same error so often committed by his fathers, of taking into their own hands the work that God had promised to do. It was not God’s will to deliver His people by warfare, as Moses thought, but by His own mighty power, that the glory might be ascribed to Him alone.”Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 247 · PP 247.3, excerpt

She does not question Moses’ love for his people, only his method and his timing. God would free Israel, but not by Moses’ own strength.

A right cause still needs God’s way and God’s time.

The next day, Moses found two Hebrew men fighting and asked the one in the wrong, “Why are you beating up your friend?” Exodus 2:13, NLT, excerpt The answer cut deep: “The man replied, ‘Who appointed you to be our prince and judge? Are you going to kill me as you killed that Egyptian yesterday?’ Then Moses was afraid, thinking, ‘Everyone knows what I did.’” Exodus 2:14, NLT

The people he hoped to lead did not want him, and his secret was out. “And sure enough, Pharaoh heard what had happened, and he tried to kill Moses. But Moses fled from Pharaoh and went to live in the land of Midian.” Exodus 2:15, NLT, excerpt Midian was a dry land far from the palaces of Egypt. The prince had become a fugitive.

Yet his desire to protect the weak was still alive. At a well in Midian, a priest’s daughters were being driven from the water: “But some other shepherds came and chased them away. So Moses jumped up and rescued the girls from the shepherds. Then he drew water for their flocks.” Exodus 2:17, NLT His sense of justice had not died. It needed to be shaped by God.

Ellen White reminds us that God was still guiding Moses: “The Lord directed his course, and he found a home with Jethro, the priest and prince of Midian, who was also a worshiper of God.” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 247 · PP 247.2, excerpt (Exodus also calls him Reuel.) Moses married his daughter Zipporah, and their son’s name reveals how Moses felt: “Later she gave birth to a son, and Moses named him Gershom, for he explained, ‘I have been a foreigner in a foreign land.’” Exodus 2:22, NLT

Many of us know that feeling: being far from where we expected to be, wondering whether one mistake has closed every door.

A failure may take us far from where we planned, but never beyond God’s reach.

Stephen tells us how long that exile lasted: “Forty years later, in the desert near Mount Sinai, an angel appeared to Moses in the flame of a burning bush.” Acts 7:30, NLT Forty years can look like a wasted life. Ellen White saw those years very differently:

“Man would have dispensed with that long period of toil and obscurity, deeming it a great loss of time. But Infinite Wisdom called him who was to become the leader of his people to spend forty years in the humble work of a shepherd. The habits of caretaking, of self-forgetfulness and tender solicitude for his flock, thus developed, would prepare him to become the compassionate, longsuffering shepherd of Israel.”Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 247 · PP 247.4, excerpt

By “Infinite Wisdom” she is referring to God Himself. The man who once tried to win freedom in an afternoon now spent his days leading sheep, protecting the weak ones, and waiting. In another book, she writes: “Apparently cut off forever from his life’s mission, he was receiving the discipline essential for its fulfillment.” Education, p. 62 · Ed 62.3, excerpt

The change was real. Years later, the Bible says: “(Now Moses was very humble—more humble than any other person on earth.)” Numbers 12:3, NLT The man who once struck in anger would one day tell a frightened nation at the Red Sea, “The LORD himself will fight for you. Just stay calm.” Exodus 14:14, NLT

Some of us are in a season like Midian: a delay, an unnoticed role, or a closed door after a mistake. Such a season need not be wasted. God may be teaching us lessons in patience, humility, and trust that cannot be learned in a hurry: “Be still in the presence of the LORD, and wait patiently for him to act.” Psalm 37:7, NLT, excerpt

God does not waste the wilderness.

Meanwhile, the suffering in Egypt went on: “Years passed, and the king of Egypt died. But the Israelites continued to groan under their burden of slavery. They cried out for help, and their cry rose up to God.” Exodus 2:23, NLT Then come the chapter’s most hopeful words: “God heard their groaning, and he remembered his covenant promise to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.” Exodus 2:24, NLT “He looked down on the people of Israel and knew it was time to act.” Exodus 2:25, NLT

God heard. God remembered His covenant, the binding promise He had made to their ancestors. God saw, and God knew it was time. Ellen White notes that Moses, too, had been bringing Israel’s need to God in the desert: “his prayers for Israel ascended by day and by night.” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 251 · PP 251.1, excerpt

At the burning bush, God spoke to him: “I have certainly seen the oppression of my people in Egypt. I have heard their cries of distress because of their harsh slave drivers. Yes, I am aware of their suffering.” Exodus 3:7, NLT, excerpt “So I have come down to rescue them from the power of the Egyptians” Exodus 3:8, NLT, excerpt Then He added, “Now go, for I am sending you to Pharaoh.” Exodus 3:10, NLT, excerpt

The once self-confident prince now asked, “Who am I to appear before Pharaoh?” Exodus 3:11, NLT, excerpt God answered, “I will be with you.” Exodus 3:12, NLT, excerpt Stephen points out how remarkable this was: “So God sent back the same man his people had previously rejected when they demanded, ‘Who made you a ruler and judge over us?’ Through the angel who appeared to him in the burning bush, God sent Moses to be their ruler and savior.” Acts 7:35, NLT

Ellen White does not excuse what Moses did, but she sees God’s hand even here: “Yet even this rash act was overruled by God to accomplish His purposes.” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 247 · PP 247.3, excerpt By “overruled” she means that God turned the act to serve His plan. That does not make the wrong right, but our failures do not have the final word.

The deliverance Moses could not force came when God said it was time.

Moses’ story points us to a greater Deliverer. Like Moses, Jesus came to rescue His own people, and like Moses, He was rejected: “He came to his own people, and even they rejected him.” John 1:11, NLT But Jesus never sought freedom by force. When a follower drew a sword at His arrest, “‘Put away your sword,’ Jesus told him.” Matthew 26:52, NLT, excerpt Instead, He chose the path of the cross.

And He came at exactly the right time: “But when the right time came, God sent his Son, born of a woman, subject to the law.” Galatians 4:4, NLT “God sent him to buy freedom for us who were slaves to the law, so that he could adopt us as his very own children.” Galatians 4:5, NLT The freedom Jesus brings goes beyond the escape from Egypt: freedom from sin and a welcome into God’s family.

When we see injustice, we should care, because God cares. But we are called to seek His way: to pray, to seek wise counsel, to act with patience and peace, and not to take into our own hands what belongs to God. And if, like Moses, we have already acted wrongly, there is hope. The God who met a failed and frightened shepherd at the burning bush still calls people who have failed.

One zealous heart. One long wilderness. One faithful God who still calls.

Reflect

  1. Is there a situation where I am sure my cause is right, but I am tempted to act in my own strength instead of seeking God’s way?
  2. If I am in a “Midian season” of waiting or obscurity, what might God be teaching me there?
  3. Have I allowed a past failure to convince me that God can no longer use me?

Key Takeaways

  1. A good cause needs God’s way. Moses truly cared for his people, but he tried to deliver them by his own strength, and his plan collapsed the next day. Exodus 2:12 and Acts 7:25 · quoted above
  2. God uses the wilderness. The forty years in Midian were not wasted; they shaped him into a humble, patient shepherd for God’s people. Acts 7:30 · quoted above
  3. God acts at the right time. God heard His people, remembered His promise, and sent back the man who had failed, assuring him of His presence. Exodus 2:24–25; 3:7, 12 · quoted above

A prayer for today

Heavenly Father, thank You that You hear the cries of those who suffer and that You never forget Your promises. Give us hearts that care about injustice, as Moses did, and the humility to seek Your way and wait for Your time. Where we have run ahead of You, forgive us and teach us the lessons of the wilderness. Thank You for Jesus, who came at the right time to set us free and who chose the cross rather than the sword. When we feel that our failures have disqualified us, remind us that You still call and still say, “I will be with you.” In Jesus’ name, Amen.

— Dr. Davies Rene Segera
