Today's Devotion

God’s Promise Does Not Need Our Deception

Key Verse
“But Isaac said, ‘Your brother was here, and he tricked me. He has taken away your blessing.’”
Genesis 27:35 (NLT)
“Trust in the Lord with all your heart; do not depend on your own understanding. Seek his will in all you do, and he will show you which path to take.”
Proverbs 3:5–6 (NLT)

Can a good goal ever make a dishonest method right? Genesis 27 brings that question into the heart of one family. Before the twins were born, the Lord had told Rebekah, “your older son will serve your younger son.” Genesis 25:23 (NLT) Years later Isaac, “old and turning blind,” Genesis 27:1 (NLT) sent Esau to hunt, promising to bless him “before I die.” Genesis 27:4 (NLT) Rebekah overheard, and she moved at once: “Go out to the flocks, and bring me two fine young goats. … Then take the food to your father so he can eat it and bless you before he dies.” Genesis 27:9–10 (NLT) She held a true word from God, and she reached for a plan God had never given. Ellen G. White helps us see the reasoning behind it: Rebekah “was confident that it was contrary to what God had revealed as His will. … She had in vain tried the effect of reasoning with Isaac, and she determined to resort to stratagem” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 180 · PP 180.1. The oracle said what God would do; it said nothing about goats, borrowed clothes, or deceiving a blind father. The right goal does not make every route to it right.

Jacob hesitated—but listen to what he feared: “What if my father touches me? He’ll see that I’m trying to trick him, and then he’ll curse me instead of blessing me.” Genesis 27:12 (NLT) His mother answered, “Then let the curse fall on me, my son! Just do what I tell you.” Genesis 27:13 (NLT) Her words were a mother’s assurance, not a divine ruling; no one can take over another person’s responsibility by offering to. Ellen G. White tells us that “the thought of deceiving his father caused him great distress. … But his scruples were overborne, and he proceeded to carry out his mother’s suggestions” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 180 · PP 180.2. Hesitation is not refusal. Dressed in his brother’s clothes, with goatskin on his arms and neck (Genesis 27:15–16), Jacob carried the meal in himself, and each question needed another lie. “Who are you—Esau or Jacob?” “It’s Esau, your firstborn son.” Genesis 27:18–19 (NLT) “How did you find it so quickly, my son?” “The Lord your God put it in my path!” Jacob replied. Genesis 27:20 (NLT) Scripture records Jacob’s claim about the Lord; it does not confirm it—the reader already knows the meal came from the flock and from Rebekah’s hands. Ellen G. White adds, “It was not his intention to utter a direct falsehood, but once in the presence of his father he seemed to have gone too far to retreat, and he obtained by fraud the coveted blessing.” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 180 · PP 180.2 Her planning does not erase his answers, and his answers do not erase her planning. Invoking God’s name does not make a falsehood true.

The plan worked: Isaac “was finally convinced, and he blessed his son.” Genesis 27:27 (NLT) Then Esau returned. “Isaac began to tremble uncontrollably” Genesis 27:33 (NLT); Esau “let out a loud and bitter cry” Genesis 27:34 (NLT); and Isaac named it: “Your brother was here, and he tricked me. He has taken away your blessing.” Genesis 27:35 (NLT) Isaac’s blindness was a vulnerability, not a fault, and his favouritism (Genesis 25:28) does not make the deception acceptable. Esau’s earlier contempt for his birthright (Genesis 25:34) does not make his grief irrelevant, and his grief does not justify his threat to kill his brother (Genesis 27:41). One person’s wrongdoing does not cancel another’s responsibility. Yet Isaac said, “that blessing must stand!” Genesis 27:33 (NLT) and later, knowing everything, blessed Jacob openly with “the blessings he promised to Abraham.” Genesis 28:4 (NLT) God’s purpose continued; that is not the same as approval. Ellen G. White holds both truths together—Isaac saw “God’s providence” in the outcome, and yet it was “the sin of which Jacob was now guilty” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 181 · PP 181.1—and she states the verdict: “Jacob’s course in this was not approbated by God. Rebekah and Jacob should have waited for God to bring about His own purposes in His own way, and in His own time, instead of trying to bring about the foretold events by the aid of deception.” The Story of Redemption, p. 88 · SR 88.2 “Approbated” means approved. The cost reached the relationships. Rebekah expected a short absence (Genesis 27:44), but Ellen G. White records that she “never saw his face again,” and that Jacob “had sinned against his father, his brother, his own soul, and against God. In one short hour he had made work for a lifelong repentance” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 180 · PP 180.3. A plan can reach its immediate goal and still be a failure of faithfulness.

So the story turns to us. Rebekah and Jacob wanted something good, and so do we when we exaggerate a report, hide a material fact, or press someone we love toward “God’s will” by manipulation. Am I trying to secure a good outcome through a method that requires me to disobey God? Trust looks different: “Trust in the Lord with all your heart; do not depend on your own understanding. Seek his will in all you do, and he will show you which path to take.” Proverbs 3:5–6 (NLT) Trust is not passivity—honest conversation, diligent preparation, wise counsel and lawful advocacy are faithful action; deception, coercion and manipulation are not. We do not have to know how God will resolve a situation in order to know that dishonesty is not our calling. And if you recognise your own hand in a plan that worked but was not right, the story does not end in the tent. Jacob fled alone, and at Bethel God met him with a promise: “I am with you, and I will protect you wherever you go. … I will not leave you until I have finished giving you everything I have promised you.” Genesis 28:15 (NLT) Ellen G. White describes him confessing his sin “with weeping and deep humiliation” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 183 · PP 183.2 and then: “But God did not forsake Jacob. His mercy was still extended to His erring, distrustful servant. The Lord compassionately revealed just what Jacob needed—a Saviour.” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 183 · PP 183.3 That Saviour is Jesus, who “never sinned, nor ever deceived anyone” and who “personally carried our sins in his body on the cross so that we can be dead to sin and live for what is right.” 1 Peter 2:22, 24 (NLT) Grace does not rename deception as faith; it meets the person who has deceived and opens a way of repentance, forgiveness and new obedience. Come to Christ truthfully: stop defending the wrong, receive His grace, and take the next faithful step toward repair. Trust Christ with the outcome, and let Him make you truthful in the process—God’s promise does not need our deception.

Key Takeaways

  1. The right goal does not make every route to it right. The Lord had said, “your older son will serve your younger son.” Genesis 25:23 (NLT) Ellen White tells us Rebekah “determined to resort to stratagem” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 180 · PP 180.1—a true promise became the excuse for a plan God never gave.
  2. Invoking God’s name does not make a falsehood true. “The Lord your God put it in my path!” Genesis 27:20 (NLT) Scripture records the claim; it does not confirm it. Ellen White says Jacob “obtained by fraud the coveted blessing” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 180 · PP 180.2.
  3. A plan can reach its immediate goal and still be a failure of faithfulness. “Your brother was here, and he tricked me.” Genesis 27:35 (NLT) God’s purpose continued, yet “Jacob’s course in this was not approbated by God” The Story of Redemption, p. 88 · SR 88.2.
  4. Trust Christ with the outcome. “Trust in the Lord with all your heart; do not depend on your own understanding.” Proverbs 3:5 (NLT) Jesus “never sinned, nor ever deceived anyone” 1 Peter 2:22 (NLT) and carried our sins—God’s promise does not need our deception.
