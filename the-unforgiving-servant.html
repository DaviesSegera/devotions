The Unforgiving Servant: When Mercy Received Becomes Mercy Shared

Matthew 18:21–35 · 10 min read

By Dr. Davies Rene Segera · 2026-10-22

Jesus tells of a servant forgiven an enormous debt who refuses mercy to another. Reflect on God’s grace, forgiving from the heart, and extending compassion while seeking safety and wise counsel where needed.

Key Verse
“Shouldn’t you have mercy on your fellow servant, just as I had mercy on you?”
Matthew 18:33 (NLT)

Have you ever forgiven someone, only to wake the next morning and feel the hurt all over again? Peter once asked Jesus how often he should forgive. Jesus answered with a story about a king, a servant, and a debt that could never be repaid.

Peter asked, “Lord, how often should I forgive someone who sins against me? Seven times?” Matthew 18:21, NLT, excerpt Jesus’ answer went far beyond Peter’s suggestion: “No, not seven times,” Jesus replied, “but seventy times seven!” Matthew 18:22, NLT

Ellen G. White explains that the teachers of Peter’s day had set a limit: “The rabbis limited the exercise of forgiveness to three offenses.” Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 243 · COL 243.1, excerpt Peter was willing to forgive more often, but Jesus taught forgiveness without a limit: “But Christ taught that we are never to become weary of forgiving.” Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 243 · COL 243.1, excerpt

Jesus taught Peter to forgive without keeping count.

To help Peter understand, Jesus told this story: “Therefore, the Kingdom of Heaven can be compared to a king who decided to bring his accounts up to date with servants who had borrowed money from him.” Matthew 18:23, NLT “In the process, one of his debtors was brought in who owed him millions of dollars.” Matthew 18:24, NLT The debt was far beyond anything the servant could repay.

The king’s order was severe: “He couldn’t pay, so his master ordered that he be sold—along with his wife, his children, and everything he owned—to pay the debt.” Matthew 18:25, NLT “But the man fell down before his master and begged him, ‘Please, be patient with me, and I will pay it all.’” Matthew 18:26, NLT Even then, the servant still believed he could repay everything.

Ellen White explains what his promise reveals: “When the debtor pleaded with his lord for mercy, he had no true sense of the greatness of his debt. He did not realize his helplessness.” Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 245 · COL 245.3, excerpt

The servant asked for time to pay. The king gave him something far greater: “Then his master was filled with pity for him, and he released him and forgave his debt.” Matthew 18:27, NLT

Ellen White helps us see Jesus in the merciful king: “The pardon granted by this king represents a divine forgiveness of all sin. Christ is represented by the king, who, moved with compassion, forgave the debt of his servant.” Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 244 · COL 244.3, excerpt Paul describes this same mercy at work in our lives: “But God showed his great love for us by sending Christ to die for us while we were still sinners.” Romans 5:8, NLT

The king did not give the servant more time. He cancelled the whole debt.

The story then takes a painful turn: “But when the man left the king, he went to a fellow servant who owed him a few thousand dollars. He grabbed him by the throat and demanded instant payment.” Matthew 18:28, NLT This was a real debt, though small compared with the one he had just been forgiven.

“His fellow servant fell down before him and begged for a little more time. ‘Be patient with me, and I will pay it,’ he pleaded.” Matthew 18:29, NLT “But his creditor wouldn’t wait. He had the man arrested and put in prison until the debt could be paid in full.” Matthew 18:30, NLT

As Ellen White points out, the forgiven man heard an appeal much like his own: “His debtor made an appeal similar to that which he himself had made to the king, but without a similar result. He who had so recently been forgiven was not tenderhearted and pitiful.” Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 245 · COL 245.2, excerpt Here, the older word “pitiful” means full of pity or compassion.

We may recognise something of ourselves in this servant. When someone hurts us, the wrong can fill our thoughts, and we can lose sight of the mercy we have received. Ellen White brings the comparison into focus:

“Their own sins against God, compared with their brother’s sins against them, are as ten thousand talents to one hundred pence—nearly one million to one; yet they dare to be unforgiving.”Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 245 · COL 245.3, excerpt

Here, “pence” is the older Bible word for the coins owed by the fellow servant. Our hurt is real, yet God’s mercy towards us is greater still.

When we forget how much we have been forgiven, the wrongs of others begin to look larger than they are.

The other servants had seen what happened: “When some of the other servants saw this, they were very upset. They went to the king and told him everything that had happened.” Matthew 18:31, NLT The king’s response takes us to the heart of the story: “Then the king called in the man he had forgiven and said, ‘You evil servant! I forgave you that tremendous debt because you pleaded with me. Shouldn’t you have mercy on your fellow servant, just as I had mercy on you?’” Matthew 18:32–33, NLT

Ellen White draws out the lesson in the king’s question, quoting it from the King James Version:

“But the great lesson of the parable lies in the contrast between God’s compassion and man’s hardheartedness; in the fact that God’s forgiving mercy is to be the measure of our own. ‘Shouldest not thou also have had compassion on thy fellowservant, even as I had pity on thee?’”Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 251 · COL 251.3

The story ends with a solemn warning: “Then the angry king sent the man to prison to be tortured until he had paid his entire debt.” Matthew 18:34, NLT Jesus applied it directly to His listeners: “That’s what my heavenly Father will do to you if you refuse to forgive your brothers and sisters from your heart.” Matthew 18:35, NLT He taught the same truth in the Sermon on the Mount: “If you forgive those who sin against you, your heavenly Father will forgive you.” Matthew 6:14, NLT “But if you refuse to forgive others, your Father will not forgive your sins.” Matthew 6:15, NLT Ellen White states it plainly: “He who refuses to forgive is thereby casting away his own hope of pardon.” Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 247 · COL 247.1, excerpt

Christians understand this part of the parable in different ways. Ellen White sees it as a warning that a person can turn away from mercy already received: “It is true that he may once have received forgiveness; but his unmerciful spirit shows that he now rejects God’s pardoning love.” Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 251 · COL 251.2, excerpt Many Christians in the Reformed tradition believe that God does not withdraw forgiveness He has truly given; they understand the story as a serious warning to those who have been forgiven. Whatever our understanding of this detail, Jesus’ words call each of us to examine our own hearts honestly.

Ellen White also reminds us where forgiveness begins:

“We are not forgiven because we forgive, but as we forgive. The ground of all forgiveness is found in the unmerited love of God, but by our attitude toward others we show whether we have made that love our own.”Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 251 · COL 251.4, excerpt

“Unmerited” means undeserved. We cannot earn God’s forgiveness by forgiving others. His love comes first; the way we treat others reveals whether we have welcomed that love into our hearts.

We do not forgive to earn God’s mercy. We forgive because His mercy has reached us.

Forgiving “from your heart” does not mean pretending that a wrong does not matter. Ellen White writes plainly, “But sin is not to be lightly regarded.” Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 248 · COL 248.2, excerpt Jesus Himself said, “If another believer sins, rebuke that person; then if there is repentance, forgive.” Luke 17:3, NLT, excerpt

Forgiveness does not require us to remain where we are being harmed, nor does it rebuild broken trust in a moment. We can forgive while seeking safety and wise counsel. As we forgive, we let go of the desire for revenge. Paul writes, “Dear friends, never take revenge. Leave that to the righteous anger of God.” Romans 12:19, NLT, excerpt

Ellen White acknowledges how deeply we may have been wounded:

“However sorely they may have wounded us, we are not to cherish our grievances and sympathize with ourselves over our injuries; but as we hope to be pardoned for our offenses against God we are to pardon all who have done evil to us.”Thoughts From the Mount of Blessing, p. 113 · MB 113.3, excerpt

Letting go of a grievance can take time. We may need to bring the same hurt to God again and again. That, too, can be part of forgiving “seventy times seven.”

Forgiveness leaves justice in God’s hands and lets go of the wish to make the other person pay.

As we bring our wounded hearts to God, His love helps us forgive where our own strength falls short. Ellen White writes, “It is the love of God that draws us unto Him, and that love cannot touch our hearts without creating love for our brethren.” Thoughts From the Mount of Blessing, p. 113 · MB 113.2, excerpt “Brethren” means our brothers and sisters.

Scripture shows us how God’s forgiveness shapes our response to others. Paul writes, “Instead, be kind to each other, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, just as God through Christ has forgiven you.” Ephesians 4:32, NLT John puts it simply: “We love each other because he loved us first.” 1 John 4:19, NLT

Ellen White describes God’s mercy flowing through us to others:

“In God’s forgiveness the heart of the erring one is drawn close to the great heart of Infinite Love. The tide of divine compassion flows into the sinner’s soul, and from him to the souls of others.”Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 251 · COL 251.1, excerpt

Perhaps you are struggling to forgive someone today. Begin by remembering the debt Jesus cancelled for you. Bring your hurt to Him honestly, and ask Him to help you show that person mercy. Your feelings may take time to follow.

The mercy we have received from Christ is the measure of the mercy we are called to share.

Reflect

  1. When I remember how others have hurt me, do I also remember how much God has forgiven me?
  2. Is there a debt I need to place in God’s hands—an apology I keep demanding, a desire for payback, or a punishment I want someone to face?
  3. Who is one person I can pray for this week, asking God to help me show them His mercy?

Key Takeaways

  1. Remember the debt Christ has cancelled. The king forgave a debt the servant could never repay. God’s mercy reaches us before we can do anything to deserve it. Matthew 18:27 · quoted above
  2. Let the mercy you receive shape the way you treat others. The king’s question calls every forgiven person to show mercy to others. Matthew 18:32–33 · quoted above
  3. Forgive from the heart, and leave justice with God. Forgiveness takes wrongdoing seriously while letting go of the desire for revenge. We may need to renew that choice again and again. Matthew 18:35 · quoted above

A prayer for today

Heavenly Father, thank You for the debt You have cancelled through Jesus Christ—a debt we could never repay. Forgive us for dwelling on the wrongs of others while forgetting Your mercy towards us. Where we have been hurt, bring healing. Where we hold on to bitterness, help us let it go and leave justice in Your hands. Give us wisdom to seek safety when we need it, and grace to forgive from the heart. Let Your love flow through us to those around us. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
