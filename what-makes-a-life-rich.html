What Makes a Life Rich?

Luke 12:13–21 · 10 min read

By Dr. Davies Rene Segera · 2026-11-18

A farmer plans bigger barns, but his life ends that night. Reflect on Jesus’ warning against greed, trusting God with tomorrow, and sharing His gifts with people in need.

Wisdom & discernmentFaith & trustLove & service
Watch the Short

Key Verse
“Then he said, ‘Beware! Guard against every kind of greed. Life is not measured by how much you own.’”
Luke 12:15 (NLT)

What makes a life rich? Few of us would say “money” out loud. Yet a bigger house or a fuller bank account can make us feel that life is richer and the future more secure. Jesus once told a story about a man who saw his life that way.

It began with an interruption. “Then someone called from the crowd, ‘Teacher, please tell my brother to divide our father’s estate with me.’” Luke 12:13, NLT Jesus declined to settle the dispute. “Jesus replied, ‘Friend, who made me a judge over you to decide such things as that?’” Luke 12:14, NLT

Ellen G. White draws our attention to what the man missed in that moment. “The gaining of the inheritance was his absorbing theme. Jesus, the King of glory, who was rich, yet for our sake became poor, was opening to him the treasures of divine love.” Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 253 · COL 253.2, excerpt An absorbing theme is what fills our thoughts. Jesus was offering him the greatest treasure of all. The man could think only of his share of the estate.

Jesus saw what was driving the quarrel. “Then he said, ‘Beware! Guard against every kind of greed. Life is not measured by how much you own.’” Luke 12:15, NLT Greed can be quiet and even look respectable. That is why Jesus warned against every kind.

Jesus used a quarrel over an inheritance to raise a deeper question. What gives a life its worth?

Jesus showed them what that kind of greed could look like. “Then he told them a story: ‘A rich man had a fertile farm that produced fine crops.’” Luke 12:16, NLT The harvest itself was a blessing. Ellen G. White reminds us who gave it. “This man had received everything from God.” Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 256 · COL 256.1, excerpt The sunshine, the rain, and the grain growing in his fields were all gifts. Moses reminded Israel of the same truth. “Remember the LORD your God. He is the one who gives you power to be successful,” Deuteronomy 8:18, NLT, excerpt

All the farmer saw was a storage problem. “He said to himself, ‘What should I do? I don’t have room for all my crops.’” Luke 12:17, NLT He soon had a plan and could already picture the years ahead. “Then he said, ‘I know! I’ll tear down my barns and build bigger ones. Then I’ll have room enough to store all my wheat and other goods. And I’ll sit back and say to myself, “My friend, you have enough stored away for years to come. Now take it easy! Eat, drink, and be merry!”’” Luke 12:18–19, NLT

Listen to how he talks. “I,” “my,” and “myself” fill his plans. There is room in his plans for bigger barns, and no room for God or for anyone else. Ellen G. White describes the life behind those words. “He lived as if there were no God, no heaven, no future life; as if everything he possessed were his own, and he owed nothing to God or man.” Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 257 · COL 257.1, excerpt

She also points out whom the farmer could have helped.

“He did not think of God, from whom all his mercies had come. He did not realize that God had made him a steward of His goods that he might help the needy.”Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 256 · COL 256.1, excerpt

A steward looks after what belongs to someone else. The farmer’s extra grain could have fed hungry families. John asks us to consider how we respond when we see someone in need. “If someone has enough money to live well and sees a brother or sister in need but shows no compassion—how can God’s love be in that person?” 1 John 3:17, NLT

The harvest was a gift. The trouble began when the farmer treated it as his alone.

The farmer had been talking to himself. Then God spoke. “But God said to him, ‘You fool! You will die this very night. Then who will get everything you worked for?’” Luke 12:20, NLT He had counted on years of ease. His life would end that night.

Ellen G. White describes how quickly his wealth lost its value.

“Here is a demand that money cannot supply. The wealth he has treasured can purchase no reprieve. In one moment that which he has toiled through his whole life to secure becomes worthless to him.”Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 258 · COL 258.2, excerpt

Even with his barns full, the farmer couldn’t buy himself one more day.

James reminds us how little we know about tomorrow. “How do you know what your life will be like tomorrow? Your life is like the morning fog—it’s here a little while, then it’s gone.” James 4:14, NLT We can make plans with humility and entrust them to God. James shows us how to put that trust into words. “What you ought to say is, ‘If the Lord wants us to, we will live and do this or that.’” James 4:15, NLT

In all his planning for the years ahead, the farmer had never prepared to meet God.

Jesus closed the story with one sentence. “Yes, a person is a fool to store up earthly wealth but not have a rich relationship with God.” Luke 12:21, NLT Many older translations say “rich toward God.” The farmer had filled his barns without giving a thought to his relationship with God.

Hard work and careful saving have their place. Paul told Timothy to teach people with plenty where to put their trust and how to use what they had. “Teach those who are rich in this world not to be proud and not to trust in their money, which is so unreliable. Their trust should be in God, who richly gives us all we need for our enjoyment.” 1 Timothy 6:17, NLT “Tell them to use their money to do good. They should be rich in good works and generous to those in need, always being ready to share with others.” 1 Timothy 6:18, NLT “By doing this they will be storing up their treasure as a good foundation for the future so that they may experience true life.” 1 Timothy 6:19, NLT

We grow rich toward God as we put our trust in Him and share His gifts with others. Ellen G. White shows what the farmer lost when he kept everything for himself. “In living for self he has rejected that divine love which would have flowed out in mercy to his fellow men. Thus he has rejected life. For God is love, and love is life.” Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 258 · COL 258.3, excerpt

A rich life is full of trust in God and love for others.

After the parable, Jesus turned to His disciples and spoke to their everyday worries. “That is why I tell you not to worry about everyday life—whether you have enough food to eat or enough clothes to wear.” Luke 12:22, NLT, excerpt He had a tender word for them, too. “So don’t be afraid, little flock. For it gives your Father great happiness to give you the Kingdom.” Luke 12:32, NLT Jesus invites us to find our security in a Father who is glad to give.

When we trust our Father to provide, we can give freely too. “Sell your possessions and give to those in need. This will store up treasure for you in heaven! And the purses of heaven never get old or develop holes. Your treasure will be safe; no thief can steal it and no moth can destroy it.” Luke 12:33, NLT “Wherever your treasure is, there the desires of your heart will also be.” Luke 12:34, NLT How we use what we have shows what our hearts treasure.

Jesus Himself shows us what it means to be rich toward God. Paul writes, “You know the generous grace of our Lord Jesus Christ. Though he was rich, yet for your sakes he became poor, so that by his poverty he could make you rich.” 2 Corinthians 8:9, NLT The farmer wanted to keep everything for himself. Jesus gave Himself for us. Ellen G. White sets these two ways of life side by side.

“To live for self is to perish. Covetousness, the desire of benefit for self’s sake, cuts the soul off from life. It is the spirit of Satan to get, to draw to self. It is the spirit of Christ to give, to sacrifice self for the good of others.”Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 259 · COL 259.1, excerpt

So what makes a life rich? We may have much or little. Either way, Jesus invites us to trust Him, receive His grace, and hold His gifts with open hands. Who might we bless today with what He has already given us?

Life is not measured by how much we own, but by whether we are rich toward God.

Reflect

  1. What has been my “absorbing theme” lately? How much room does it leave for God and the people around me?
  2. When God gives me more than I need, what do I think of first? Who might need what I have to spare?
  3. What is one way I can grow richer toward God this week?

Key Takeaways

  1. Life is not measured by possessions. Jesus looked past a family quarrel and warned against every kind of greed, even the kind that looks respectable. Luke 12:13–15 · quoted above
  2. Every harvest is a gift to share. The farmer received his harvest from God. He could have used the extra grain to feed people in need. Luke 12:16–19 · quoted above
  3. True riches are found in God. Our Father is glad to give us the Kingdom, so we can trust Him, give freely, and store up treasure that lasts. Luke 12:32–34 · quoted above

A prayer for today

Heavenly Father, thank You for every good gift, from the food on our tables to the grace that saves us. Forgive us for measuring our lives by what we own and making plans that leave You and others out. Guard our hearts against every kind of greed. Teach us to trust You with tomorrow, to notice who needs help, and to share gladly what You have placed in our hands. Thank You for Jesus, who became poor so that we could be rich. Make us rich toward You and let our lives show His generous love. In Jesus’ name, Amen.

— Dr. Davies Rene Segera
