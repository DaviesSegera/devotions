Holding On to God at Peniel

Genesis 32:22–32 · 6 min read

By Dr. Davies Rene Segera · 2026-09-15

Jacob’s night at Peniel invites us to bring our fears to God, persist in prayer, and receive the grace that changes how we live.

PrayerFaith & trustForgiveness & graceHumility & obedience

Key Verse
“I will not let you go unless you bless me.”
Genesis 32:26 (NLT, excerpt)
“From now on you will be called Israel, because you have fought with God and with men and have won.”
Genesis 32:28 (NLT, excerpt)

Twenty years earlier Jacob had run for his life. Now he was walking back — because God had told him to: “Return to the land of your father and grandfather and to your relatives there, and I will be with you.” Genesis 31:3 (NLT) The fear that fills Genesis 32 is not that of a man who has wandered off, but of a man doing the right thing on a hard road.

Somewhere ahead was Esau, the brother he had cheated of their father’s blessing, who had once sworn to kill him Genesis 27:41. His messengers came back with a single sentence: “he is already on his way to meet you—with an army of 400 men!” Genesis 32:6 (NLT, excerpt) “Jacob was terrified at the news.” Genesis 32:7 (NLT, excerpt) Scripture does not scold him for that; it records what he did with his fear. He prayed — and the prayer comes before anything that happens at the river, because he did not discover God that night; he had known Him for years. His prayer names God’s instruction, his unworthiness, his need and God’s promise Genesis 32:9–12. He also sent gifts ahead and divided his camp; Genesis does not call those arrangements unbelief.

Jacob walked toward his fear on a road God had told him to take.

Then he was alone. He took his family across the Jabbok, sent his possessions over, “and a man came and wrestled with him until the dawn began to break.” Genesis 32:24 (NLT, excerpt) The man came to Jacob, not Jacob to him. When the man “saw that he would not win the match, he touched Jacob’s hip and wrenched it out of its socket.” Genesis 32:25 (NLT, excerpt) And at the moment he was most outmatched, Jacob said the sentence this devotion hangs on: “I will not let you go unless you bless me.” Genesis 32:26 (NLT, excerpt)

Hosea told us what that night sounded like: “He wept and pleaded for a blessing from him.” Hosea 12:4 (NLT, excerpt) Tears and pleading — not leverage, not technique. Nothing here makes an all-night vigil, a flood of tears or a particular posture the price of being heard. A believer who cannot kneel, a child with a simple prayer, a person with only one word left — each is holding on in the sense that matters.

Persistent prayer is not force applied to God; it is need brought to mercy.

The man does not concede. He asks a question: “What is your name?” Genesis 32:27 (NLT, excerpt) It lands where his history lives: his name was the problem — the NLT notes that Jacob sounds like the Hebrew words for “heel” and “deceiver.” When he answers with one word, he says the truest thing about himself that he knows. Sometimes honesty is a single word.

Then comes the gift. “From now on you will be called Israel, because you have fought with God and with men and have won.” Genesis 32:28 (NLT, excerpt) The NLT note explains that Israel means “God fights.” Jacob did not announce a new identity; it was given, and then “he blessed Jacob there.” Genesis 32:29 (NLT, excerpt) He named the place Peniel, “which means ‘face of God’” Genesis 32:30 (NLT, excerpt) — Peniel the name of the place, Israel the name of the man — and “the sun was rising as Jacob left Peniel, and he was limping.” Genesis 32:31 (NLT, excerpt) So what does “won” mean, on a night when one touch disabled him? Ellen G. White, who tells us whom Jacob held — “It was Christ, ‘the Angel of the covenant,’ who had revealed Himself to Jacob” PP 196.3 — answers directly:

“Through humiliation, repentance, and self-surrender, this sinful, erring mortal prevailed with the Majesty of heaven. He had fastened his trembling grasp upon the promises of God, and the heart of Infinite Love could not turn away the sinner’s plea.” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 197 · PP 197.1

What Jacob could not seize by strength, God gave him as a blessing.

Watch what that does to him by morning. Genesis 33 opens with Esau and his four hundred men in sight, and the changed man goes out ahead of his family — limping — and bows seven times. When Esau runs to embrace him and they weep, Jacob says something no one who has spent a night at Peniel could say by accident: “It is like seeing the face of God!” Genesis 33:10 (NLT, excerpt) A man who has received undeserved mercy has begun to see mercy in another face.

The application is modest. Grace received should change how we approach those we have wronged: admitting responsibility, apologising without conditions, making repair where possible, asking for wise help. It never means we control the outcome. Esau embraced Jacob; Scripture does not promise that every estranged relationship resolves like this one, or on this timetable, and reconciliation never requires unsafe contact or covering up what was done. Nor did the new name make Jacob flawless — Scripture keeps calling him Jacob for the rest of his life. A changed life begins in grace and continues in repentance, with “an advocate who pleads our case before the Father” 1 John 2:1 (NLT, excerpt).

So bring God the fear you cannot resolve, and bring it boldly, because of who meets you: “let us come boldly to the throne of our gracious God. There we will receive his mercy, and we will find grace to help us when we need it most.” Hebrews 4:16 (NLT, excerpt) And the blessing Jacob pleaded for comes to us as it came to him, never as something our struggling earns: “we have been made right in God’s sight by faith… we have peace with God because of what Jesus Christ our Lord has done for us.” Romans 5:1 (NLT, excerpt) Jacob limped into the sunrise blessed, forgiven, and on his way to meet his brother.

Bring your fear, turn from sin, trust Christ’s mercy, and take your next step with God.

Reflect

  1. What fear or burden will I bring to God today instead of carrying it alone?
  2. What am I still trying to control that I need to entrust to God?
  3. Will I keep coming to God and allow His grace to change me?

Key Takeaways

  1. Jacob walked toward his fear on a road God had told him to take. “Jacob was terrified at the news.” Genesis 32:7 (NLT, excerpt) He prayed before the wrestling began, naming God’s instruction, his own unworthiness, his need and God’s promise. Fear brought honestly to God is not a failure of faith.
  2. Persistent prayer is not force applied to God; it is need brought to mercy. “I will not let you go unless you bless me.” Genesis 32:26 (NLT, excerpt) Hosea remembers the tears and the pleading Hosea 12:4; Ellen White describes “the assurance of one who confesses his own unworthiness, yet trusts the faithfulness of a covenant-keeping God” PP 196.3. Not stamina — dependence.
  3. What Jacob could not seize by strength, God gave him as a blessing. God gives the new name; Jacob does not manufacture it. Israel is the name of the man, Peniel the name of the place, and he left at sunrise limping. “It was by self-surrender and confiding faith that Jacob gained what he had failed to gain by conflict in his own strength.” PP 202.4
  4. Bring your fear, turn from sin, trust Christ’s mercy, and take your next step with God. Mercy received changes how we meet the people we have wronged — without controlling their response. Our confidence is Christ: “let us come boldly to the throne of our gracious God.” Hebrews 4:16 (NLT, excerpt)
