When Pride Bows to the Word of God

2 Kings 5 · 20 min read

By Dr. Davies Rene Segera

Naaman’s encounter with God’s word in 2 Kings 5 invites us to lay down pride and receive grace with humble faith.

Humility & obedienceForgiveness & graceFaith & trust

Key Verse
“Sir, if the prophet had told you to do something very difficult … So Naaman went down … and dipped himself seven times …”
2 Kings 5:13–14 (NLT)

2 Kings 5 opens by placing two realities side by side. Naaman was commander of the army of Aram, respected by his king, celebrated for military success and known for courage. Scripture says that “through him the LORD had given Aram great victories” 2 Kings 5:1 (NLT), reminding us that God's sovereign activity was never confined to Israel's borders. By every public measure, Naaman was a great man. His rank could move soldiers, his reputation could open royal doors, and his wealth could finance a journey across national boundaries.

Yet Scripture says, “But though Naaman was a mighty warrior, he suffered from leprosy.” 2 Kings 5:1 (NLT) His serious condition was something none of those advantages could cure. The Hebrew term traditionally rendered as leprosy can describe several serious skin conditions, so we should not force a modern diagnosis upon the text. Nor does the chapter say that Naaman's illness was caused by his pride, by some hidden sin or by deficient faith. His condition is not permission to treat physical illness as a sign of moral inferiority.

It simply reveals a truth every human life eventually encounters: there are needs before which status becomes powerless. The uniform still fitted, the victories still stood and the king's admiration remained, but beneath all that public honour was a private vulnerability Naaman could neither command nor conceal. Perhaps that is why the story speaks so tenderly to people who appear capable while carrying burdens few others can see. A person may lead an organisation, provide for a family, solve difficult problems and still lie awake before a need that money, education, influence and determination cannot reach.

Competence is a gift, and Scripture does not belittle Naaman's accomplishments. It does, however, expose their limits. Human greatness can take us far, but it cannot make us self-sufficient. Naaman's journey towards healing began when he became willing to receive help from beyond the world in which his authority usually worked. Long before he bowed in the Jordan, something in him had already begun to bend: this powerful commander listened when hope came from an unlikely voice. His need did not erase his dignity; it opened a door through which grace could find him.

That unlikely voice belonged to a young Israelite girl whose name Scripture does not record. Aramean raiders had taken her from her homeland; Scripture records that “among their captives was a young girl” 2 Kings 5:2 (NLT), and that she had been given to Naaman's wife as a maid. The Bible does not soften the injustice of that displacement, and neither should we. She had not chosen this household, this country or this work. Faithfulness in captivity does not make captivity good, and God's use of her witness does not mean He approved the violence that carried her away.

Yet suffering had not erased her memory of God, and captivity had not extinguished her compassion. She knew of Elisha in Samaria, and she believed that the God whose prophet lived there could help the man whose household now held her. She could have remained silent. Instead, she spoke to her mistress: “I wish my master would go to see the prophet in Samaria.” 2 Kings 5:3 (NLT) In that one sentence, she directed a powerful enemy commander towards the mercy of Israel's God. In The Ministry of Healing, “The Discipline of Trial,” Ellen White observes that a difficult place may still contain someone whom our witness can help.

The statement does not ask us to pretend that every painful place is easy or that every wound should be quickly explained. It asks whether grace can still flow through us where life has not been fair. The girl had no title, platform, wealth or freedom to arrange an audience with the king. She possessed only a living confidence in God and enough compassion to speak. Yet her brief testimony set kings, servants, gifts, horses and chariots in motion. The whole visible procession began with the faith of someone the ancient world could easily overlook. God often places decisive influence in hands that history does not name.

A child may speak hope into an adult's despair. An employee may offer wisdom to a leader. A person whose circumstances seem small may point someone with enormous influence towards God. The worth of a witness is not measured by social position but by the truth and love carried in that witness. And notice Naaman's own grace here: he did not dismiss the message because it came through a captive girl. His desperation made him listen, and listening was one of the first humble acts in his transformation.

Naaman then sought help in the way a commander and royal servant naturally understood. He reported the girl's words to the king of Aram, who supplied a letter to Israel's king, and Naaman travelled with an extraordinary amount of silver, gold and fine clothing. The narrative says, “So Naaman started out, carrying as gifts 750 pounds of silver, 150 pounds of gold, and ten sets of clothing.” 2 Kings 5:4–5 (NLT) This was not merely pride. It was the language of his world: kings negotiated with kings, important matters moved through official channels, and generous gifts honoured the one who rendered a great service.

Naaman was genuinely willing to seek help. He crossed a border, accepted advice from beneath his social station and brought what he thought the occasion required. Yet he still approached grace as though it might be arranged through influence, diplomacy or payment. The king of Israel read the letter as a demand that he personally cure Naaman and tore his clothes in alarm. His cry, “Am I God, that I can give life and take it away?” 2 Kings 5:7 (NLT), exposed both the limit of royal power and his suspicion that Aram's impossible request was designed to provoke another conflict.

While two kings interpreted the situation through political power, Elisha sent word that Naaman should come so “he will learn that there is a true prophet here in Israel.” 2 Kings 5:8 (NLT) The contrast is striking. The king of Aram could authorise the journey, but he could not guarantee the result. The king of Israel occupied the throne, but he could not supply the miracle. The gifts were impressive, but they could not purchase what Naaman needed. God was quietly moving the search away from royal leverage and towards His word. We often bring to God the currencies that have worked elsewhere: achievement, connections, effort, promises about what we will do if He answers, or a careful account of why we deserve His intervention.

There is wisdom in seeking appropriate human help; doctors, counsellors, pastors, family members and practical resources may all be part of God's care. But no human channel becomes God, and no gift places Him in our debt. Naaman's long journey, vulnerability and desire for healing were real. What he still had to discover was that mercy would not arrive as a reward for importance. It would come as grace, on God's terms.

Naaman arrived with horses and chariots and “waited at the door of Elisha’s house.” 2 Kings 5:9 (NLT) His rank was visible even before he spoke. He expected an encounter fitted to the seriousness of his need and the dignity of his office. Instead, Elisha remained inside and sent a messenger with a simple instruction: “Go and wash yourself seven times in the Jordan River.” 2 Kings 5:10 (NLT) The commander became angry: “I thought he would certainly come out to meet me!” 2 Kings 5:11 (NLT)

He had imagined the prophet coming out personally, invoking God's name, moving his hand dramatically over the diseased area and producing an impressive healing. The actual instruction offended not only his expectations but his sense of what an important visitor should receive. He protested, “Why shouldn’t I wash in them and be healed?” 2 Kings 5:12 (NLT), because he considered the rivers of Damascus superior to the waters of Israel and saw no reason that washing in the Jordan should accomplish what washing at home could not.

We need not caricature him. He had travelled far because he believed help might be found, and disappointment can be especially sharp when hope has cost us something. The method appeared ordinary, the messenger seemed impersonal and the river looked unworthy of the miracle. Yet Naaman's anger revealed that he was not merely asking God to heal him; he was also carrying a script for how God should do it. Most of us have one. We imagine the answer arriving through a particular person, at a particular time, with an unmistakable feeling and a result everyone can recognise.

When God's direction is quieter, slower or less impressive, we may mistake wounded expectation for spiritual discernment. Pride does not always announce itself as boasting. Sometimes it appears as offence that God has not followed our preferred script. Sometimes it prefers a difficult deed that preserves our sense of control to a simple response that requires surrender. This does not mean every disappointment is pride or every unanswered prayer results from resistance. Scripture gives no licence to blame suffering people for not obtaining the outcome they desire.

It does mean that Naaman's particular blessing stood behind a particular word he was preparing to reject. The obstacle was not the distance to the river, the number seven or the quality of the water. It was his insistence that God's answer must resemble the answer he had already imagined.

Then Naaman's officers came near. Their approach was respectful, but their counsel was brave. They reasoned that if the prophet had required some demanding achievement, Naaman would surely have attempted it; therefore, he should not reject what had been said so simply: “Go and wash and be cured!” 2 Kings 5:13 (NLT) Once again, God placed wisdom in the mouth of someone with less rank. The captive girl had told Naaman where to seek help, and now his own officers helped him remain where grace could reach him.

A wise person does not ask only whether counsel comes from someone impressive; a wise person asks whether it is true. Naaman could command these men, but in that moment he allowed them to reason with him. Humility is not pretending to have no strength; it is strength becoming teachable. The commander turned back, went down to the Jordan and followed the instruction he had received. The text does not describe what happened during the first six immersions, so we should not invent a sequence of partial changes or disappointed glances.

It tells us that after he had dipped seven times in accordance with the word given through Elisha, “his skin became as healthy as the skin of a young child …” 2 Kings 5:14 (NLT) In Prophets and Kings, chapter 20, “Naaman,” Ellen White describes faith and pride contending until faith prevailed. The Jordan possessed no magical power. Seven immersions did not form a secret technique. Naaman did not earn healing by completing a religious task, and his obedience did not make God indebted to him.

God healed him by sovereign grace and power; obedience was the response through which Naaman stopped resisting the grace freely offered. Faith took God's word seriously enough to act, even when the action contradicted his preferences. This was a specific miracle given in a specific biblical moment. It must never be turned into a promise that every sick person will receive immediate physical healing by performing the right act, reaching the right level of humility or exercising enough faith. Believers may pray earnestly, obey faithfully and still live with illness.

Naaman's account gives us no formula for controlling God. It gives us a portrait of surrender: the man accustomed to giving orders received one, the man who preferred Damascus entered the Jordan, and the man prepared to leave angry remained long enough to receive mercy.

The healed commander did not simply continue home with improved skin. “Then Naaman and his entire party went back …” 2 Kings 5:15 (NLT) and stood personally before Elisha. The man who had first arrived expecting the prophet to stand before him now stood before the prophet, and his words showed that more than his outward condition had changed. His confession was unmistakable: “Now I know that there is no God in all the world …” 2 Kings 5:15 (NLT) The miracle had become revelation; bodily relief had awakened worship and allegiance.

Naaman again offered a gift, probably from gratitude as well as from the customs of his world, but Elisha answered, “I will not accept any gifts.” 2 Kings 5:16 (NLT), and refused despite repeated urging. The refusal protected the meaning of what had happened. Israel's God was not a local wonder-worker whose favour could be bought, and Elisha was not a spiritual broker collecting payment for access to divine power. The grace that restored Naaman had been freely given. His wealth had funded the journey, but it had not caused the healing.

His gratitude was appropriate; the idea of purchase was not. Naaman's subsequent requests reveal a man whose understanding was sincere but still developing. He asked to carry earth from Israel back to Aram and declared, “From now on I will never again offer burnt offerings …” He also raised the difficult matter of his public duty when his king entered the temple of Rimmon leaning on his arm and prayed, “may the LORD pardon me when I bow, too.” 2 Kings 5:17–18 (NLT) Elisha did not sell him a rulebook or attempt to control every future decision from a distance.

His reply was brief: “Go in peace,” Elisha said. 2 Kings 5:19 (NLT) We should not make Naaman's request for earth into a doctrine the passage does not teach, nor should we flatten the complexity of a new believer returning to a pagan court. The important movement is clear: he arrived trusting rank, gifts and a planned performance; he left confessing God, carrying an altered allegiance and knowing that mercy could not be purchased. A genuine encounter with God should produce more than the relief of a problem.

We may begin by asking Him to change our circumstances, and He graciously hears us, but His deepest purpose is to bring us into truth, worship and relationship with Himself.

The chapter then turns from Naaman's generosity to Gehazi's greed. This warning belongs to the story, though it must not eclipse the central movement from pride to faith. Gehazi had witnessed Elisha refuse payment, yet he decided, “I will chase after him and get something from him.” 2 Kings 5:20 (NLT) He pursued Naaman and falsely claimed that the prophet had changed his mind because two young prophetic servants had arrived. He used Elisha's name, Naaman's gratitude and a fabricated ministry need to obtain silver and clothing.

Naaman gave readily, even sending servants to carry the goods, while Gehazi hid what he had taken and then lied again: “Where have you been, Gehazi?” “I haven’t been anywhere,” he replied. 2 Kings 5:25 (NLT) His sin was not merely wanting material provision. Scripture does not condemn honest support for ministry or the responsible use of resources. Gehazi's offence was that he commercialised a gift God had freely bestowed, misrepresented spiritual authority, manipulated a grateful recipient and concealed the proceeds. Elisha exposed the lie by asking, “Is this the time to receive money and clothing …?” 2 Kings 5:26 (NLT)

The chapter closes with the severe judgment, “you and your descendants will suffer from Naaman’s leprosy forever.” 2 Kings 5:27 (NLT) That specific judgment belongs to this inspired narrative; it does not establish a rule that illness proves personal wrongdoing, and it must never be used to stigmatise people living with disease. The moral contrast lies elsewhere. Elisha would not profit from grace; Gehazi tried to turn grace into an opportunity for gain. Naaman, the foreign outsider, became receptive and truthful about God, while someone close to the prophet chose deceit.

Spiritual proximity is not the same as spiritual transformation. Churches and ministries therefore need transparent stewardship, truthful appeals and leaders who refuse to exploit vulnerability. The gospel must never be presented as though prayer, healing, access or divine favour can be bought. God's servants may receive legitimate support, but God's mercy is never their merchandise. What He freely gives must not be enclosed behind manipulation, secrecy or personal ambition.

Centuries later, Jesus brought Naaman's story into the synagogue at Nazareth. After reading from Isaiah, He announced, “The Scripture you’ve just heard has been fulfilled this very day!” Luke 4:16–21 (NLT) Initial wonder soon gave way to resistance as His neighbours measured Him by their familiarity with His earthly family and expected signs like those reported from Capernaum. Jesus answered by recalling two occasions when God's mercy crossed the boundaries of Israel: Elijah was sent to a widow in Sidon, while in Elisha's time “the only one healed was Naaman, a Syrian.” Luke 4:27 (NLT)

Jesus was not teaching contempt for Israel or providing a basis for anti-Jewish readings. He was confronting a temptation common to every religious community: the belief that familiarity, heritage or privilege gives us a claim upon grace while people outside our circle are less worthy of it. Naaman did not receive because Syrians were superior to Israelites. He received as an outsider who became willing to trust the word that came to him. Religious privilege is a gift meant to form gratitude and witness, not entitlement.

In The Desire of Ages, chapter 24, “Is Not This the Carpenter's Son?” Ellen White presents Naaman's felt need as making him receptive to God's grace. Naaman's anger at first nearly carried him away from the river, but he allowed wise counsel to interrupt it. The anger in Nazareth hardened as the congregation attempted to drive Jesus from the town, yet “he passed right through the crowd and went on his way.” Luke 4:28–30 (NLT) The contrast is sobering. Offence need not have the final word. Pride can bow, anger can listen and an outsider can become a worshipper. But privilege can also resist the very Christ it claims to know.

Jesus' use of Naaman's story declares that God's mercy is not fenced in by nationality, social standing, background or previous religious access. Grace reaches beyond the lines we draw, and it humbles everyone who receives it because no one receives it as an entitlement. The Christ rejected in His hometown is the Saviour whose invitation still reaches both the respected commander and the unnamed servant.

Naaman's cleansing was physical, real and gracious; it should not be reduced to an illustration that makes his suffering less important. Yet his story also leads naturally to humanity's deeper need. Sin cannot be removed by rank, achievement, influence, wealth or religious effort. We cannot command a clean heart into existence, purchase forgiveness, or arrange reconciliation with God through impressive works. The New Testament announces, “he saved us, not because of the righteous things we had done …” Titus 3:5 (NLT), and, “God saved you by his grace when you believed.” Ephesians 2:8 (NLT)

That gift comes to us through Jesus Christ. At the cross, Christ bore our sin; “the blood of Jesus, his Son, cleanses us from all sin.” 1 John 1:7 (NLT) Through His resurrection He gives new life, for Scripture says, “God raised Jesus Christ from the dead.” 1 Peter 1:3 (NLT) The Jordan was not Naaman's saviour, and entering it was not payment. In the same way, Christian obedience does not purchase salvation. We obey because grace has called us, because Christ is trustworthy and because faith is willing to surrender the right to rule itself.

That distinction protects both assurance and humility. We do not stand before God presenting our obedience as a price; we come empty-handed, receive what Christ alone can give, and then follow Him in grateful allegiance. Perhaps the word confronting you today is simple: confess what you have hidden, forgive the person whose debt you keep rehearsing, seek wise help, release a dishonest advantage, make restitution, return to prayer, or obey a clear command of Scripture that pride has complicated. Test every impression by God's written word and seek mature counsel; surrender is not an invitation to abandon discernment or submit to manipulation.

And if you are carrying illness, do not hear this story as an accusation or a guarantee of immediate cure. Pray, seek appropriate care and trust God's presence without concluding that continued suffering proves failed faith. Naaman's miracle was God's particular gift to Naaman. The universal invitation is deeper and surer: everyone who comes to Christ in faith may receive forgiveness, reconciliation and new life. The turning point is not learning how to make God follow our preferred method. It is discovering that the One who meets us is wise, merciful and worthy of trust. God may not work in the manner we expect, but His word remains trustworthy.

The Jordan did not save Naaman, and his obedience did not buy the gift; God's grace did what power, wealth and reputation could never do. Our deepest cleansing still begins when pride bows to the word of Christ.

Key Takeaways

  1. Public strength can coexist with private need. Scripture says, “The king of Aram had great admiration for Naaman” 2 Kings 5:1 (NLT). His courage, influence and accomplishments were real, but they could not cure his condition. God does not shame us for reaching the limits of our ability. He meets us there and teaches us that dignity does not require self-sufficiency.
  2. God can work through a witness the world overlooks. Her confidence appears in the words, “He would heal him of his leprosy.” 2 Kings 5:2–3 (NLT) The unnamed captive girl retained faith and compassion in an unjust situation and directed Naaman towards help. Her suffering was not good, but her faithful witness was powerful. Social position does not determine spiritual usefulness.
  3. We can seek God while still trying to control His method. Naaman travelled far and brought gifts, yet when the answer did not match his expectations, “So Naaman turned and went away in a rage.” 2 Kings 5:4–12 (NLT) Honest desire and hidden pride may inhabit the same heart. Faith permits God to be wiser than our preferred script.
  4. Wise counsel does not become less wise when it comes from someone with less status. Scripture says that “his officers tried to reason with him” 2 Kings 5:13 (NLT), and he listened. Humility is not weakness; it is strength that remains teachable. One careful voice may keep us from walking away from grace.
  5. Obedience responds to grace; it does not purchase it. The Jordan had no magical power, and seven immersions were not a healing formula. He acted “as the man of God had instructed him” 2 Kings 5:14 (NLT), and God restored him. This specific miracle must never be used to blame ill people or guarantee immediate physical healing today.
  6. Grace produces worship and must never become merchandise. Elisha's position remained firm: “And though Naaman urged him to take the gift, Elisha refused.” 2 Kings 5:15–19 (NLT) Naaman returned confessing the true God. The account says, “Then he went and hid the gifts inside the house.” 2 Kings 5:20–27 (NLT) Gehazi's deceit warns against exploiting spiritual influence for personal gain. God's mercy is freely given and must be represented with truth and integrity.
  7. Christ's grace reaches outsiders and cleanses the heart. Jesus warned that “no prophet is accepted in his own hometown.” Luke 4:16–30 (NLT) He used Naaman's story to confront religious entitlement and show that privilege alone does not create receptiveness. Status, heritage and effort cannot remove sin. Forgiveness and new life come as God's gift through Jesus Christ, received by faith and followed by grateful obedience.
