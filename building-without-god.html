Today's Devotion

Building Without God

Key Verse
“Come, let’s build a great city for ourselves with a tower that reaches into the sky.”
Genesis 11:4 (NLT)

The story does not begin with a crime. It begins with a building site. “At one time all the people of the world spoke the same language and used the same words” Genesis 11:1 (NLT). They travelled east and found flat, open country: “As the people migrated to the east, they found a plain in the land of Babylonia and settled there” Genesis 11:2 (NLT). Then they got organised: “They began saying to each other, ’Let’s make bricks and harden them with fire’” Genesis 11:3 (NLT). Notice the small detail the Bible slips in. There was no good stone on that plain. So they made their own. Brick instead of rock. Tar instead of mortar. Every single thing in that project was manufactured. Nothing in it was received. Hold on to that, because the whole story is hiding in it.

Then came the plan: “Come, let’s build a great city for ourselves with a tower that reaches into the sky. This will make us famous and keep us from being scattered all over the world” Genesis 11:4 (NLT). Read that again, slowly. Two small words decide everything: for ourselves. This was not a bad building. Building is not a sin. God gave people the ability to make things, and He is pleased when we use it well. The problem was never the bricks. The problem was the address. And look at the two things they wanted out of it. First, a name — this will make us famous. Second, safety — keep us from being scattered. Fame and security. Four thousand years later, that is still the whole list.

There may have been something sadder underneath it. After the flood, God had promised that He would never destroy the earth that way again. But these builders did not believe Him. Ellen White writes that “the dwellers on the plain of Shinar disbelieved God’s covenant that He would not again bring a flood upon the earth.” By raising the tower higher than the flood waters had reached, “they thought to place themselves beyond all possibility of danger.” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 119 · PP 119.1. Sit with what that means. The tower was not only a monument. It was an insurance policy against God’s own word. They were building a place to be safe from the One who had already promised to keep them safe. That is what unbelief does to us. It makes us work very hard to provide for ourselves the very thing God has already promised to give.

Then comes one of the quietest, most humbling lines in the whole Bible: “But the Lord came down to look at the city and the tower the people were building” Genesis 11:5 (NLT). He came down. They had built the tallest thing on earth. It was designed to reach the sky. And God still had to stoop to see it. There is no anger in that sentence. There is no mockery in it either. But there is perspective, and it is humbling. Everything they were proudest of was, from where He sits, very small. Yet notice the kindness hiding in it. He came down. He did not stay far away and let them finish.

“The people are united, and they all speak the same language. After this, nothing they set out to do will be impossible for them!” Genesis 11:6 (NLT). God was not frightened of them. He was describing a danger. Human power joined together with no reference to God does not become safe. It becomes unstoppable. History has shown us that again and again since. Ellen White names their real project plainly: it was “a kingdom established for self-exaltation, but in which God was to have no rule or honor.” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 123 · PP 123.1. So God acted: “Come, let’s go down and confuse the people with different languages. Then they won’t be able to understand each other” Genesis 11:7 (NLT). It reads like judgment, and it was. But it was mercy too. Sometimes the kindest thing God can do for a person is to stop a project that would have destroyed them.

“In that way, the Lord scattered them all over the world, and they stopped building the city” Genesis 11:8 (NLT). Now look at what they lost. They wanted a name. The name they got was Babel — a word that sounds like the Hebrew for confusion. They wanted to stay together. They were scattered, which is the one thing the tower was built to prevent. Both goals collapsed. Ellen White says it in a single line: “The monument to their pride became the memorial of their folly.” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 123 · PP 123.3. But do not miss the mercy waiting in the very next chapter. One page later, God speaks to one ordinary man called Abram, and says: “I will bless you and make you famous, and you will be a blessing to others” Genesis 12:2 (NLT). Do you see it? The exact thing they tried to seize by force, God simply gives away — to a man who was not building a tower.

So the question today is not whether you are building. You are. We all are — a career, a home, some savings, a reputation, a ministry, a future for the children. God is not against any of that. Please hear that clearly. This is not a story about ambition being sinful, and it does not say that making plans is faithless. Hard work and good building can be worship. The question is only this: who is it for, and who is in it? “Unless the Lord builds a house, the work of the builders is wasted” Psalm 127:1 (NLT). James says the same thing about our plans, and he is not scolding us: “What you ought to say is, ’If the Lord wants us to, we will live and do this or that’” James 4:15 (NLT). So take one honest look at what you are building this week. Ask where God is in it — not as a decoration on the top, but down in the foundation. And then remember how the story finally ends. At Babel, God confused the languages and scattered the people. At Pentecost, He filled a room with His Spirit, and “they were bewildered to hear their own languages being spoken” Acts 2:6 (NLT). What pride broke, grace gathered back together. So build. Just build with Him.

Key Takeaways

  1. It began as a good project — building is not a sin; God gave us the ability to make things and is pleased when we use it well.
  2. Two words decide everything: “for ourselves” — the problem was never the bricks, it was the address.
  3. They wanted fame and security — a name, and a way not to be scattered; four thousand years later that is still the whole list.
  4. The tower was insurance against God’s promise — unbelief makes us work hard to provide for ourselves what God has already promised to give.
  5. “The Lord came down to look” — they built the tallest thing on earth, and He still had to stoop to see it.
  6. Stopping them was mercy, not jealousy — sometimes the kindest thing God can do is end a project that would have destroyed you.
  7. Build — but build with Him — what Babel grabbed at, God freely gave to Abram; and unless the Lord builds the house, the work is wasted.
