The Serpent, the Fruit and the First Hiding

Genesis 3:1–24 · 6 min read

By Dr. Davies Rene Segera · 2026-09-22

Genesis 3 traces temptation, shame, hiding, and blame—then reveals the God who comes looking for us and speaks hope before the sentence is finished.

Key Verse
“Then the LORD God called to the man, “Where are you?””
Genesis 3:9 (NLT)
“And I will cause hostility between you and the woman, and between your offspring and her offspring. He will strike your head, and you will strike his heel.”
Genesis 3:15 (NLT)

Before the serpent speaks, there is a gift. “The LORD God placed the man in the Garden of Eden to tend and watch over it” Genesis 2:15 (NLT). Food is everywhere; one tree is held back: “You may freely eat the fruit of every tree in the garden—except the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. If you eat its fruit, you are sure to die” 2:16–17 (NLT). A wide yes, one no, and the truth told plainly. And nothing to hide: “Now the man and his wife were both naked, but they felt no shame” 2:25 (NLT).

Then a new voice. “The serpent was the shrewdest of all the wild animals the LORD God had made. One day he asked the woman, “Did God really say you must not eat the fruit from any of the trees in the garden?”” 3:1 (NLT). God had said they could eat from every tree but one. The question makes it sound as though God had fenced off the whole garden. Nothing has been denied yet. Ellen G. White names what temptation is aiming at: “He tempts men to distrust God’s love and to doubt His wisdom.” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 54 · PP 54.3

The serpent first changed how God’s word sounded before the fruit changed hands.

The woman answers, and the fence has already moved. God said, she reports, “You must not eat it or even touch it; if you do, you will die” 3:3 (NLT, excerpt) — and Genesis 2 records no word about touching. Then the flat contradiction: “You won’t die!” the serpent replied to the woman. “God knows that your eyes will be opened as soon as you eat it, and you will be like God, knowing both good and evil.” 3:4–5 (NLT). God is now described as someone holding out on them.

“The woman was convinced. She saw that the tree was beautiful and its fruit looked delicious, and she wanted the wisdom it would give her. So she took some of the fruit and ate it. Then she gave some to her husband, who was with her, and he ate it, too” 3:6 (NLT). Beauty, hunger and wanting to be wise are not the problem; God made all three. The problem is desire cut loose from trust. And the man is no bystander — he is standing there, and he eats.

The serpent promised open eyes, and that is what they got. “At that moment their eyes were opened, and they suddenly felt shame at their nakedness. So they sewed fig leaves together to cover themselves” 3:7 (NLT). What they saw was themselves.

Sin offered them more, then left them hiding with less.

“When the cool evening breezes were blowing, the man and his wife heard the LORD God walking about in the garden. So they hid from the LORD God among the trees” 3:8 (NLT). The sound that once meant company now sends them into the bushes. Ellen G. White describes what had changed inside them: “The love and peace which had been theirs was gone, and in its place they felt a sense of sin, a dread of the future, a nakedness of soul.” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 57 · PP 57.1 Shame is not repentance. Shame says, stay out of sight. Repentance turns and walks back.

“Then the LORD God called to the man, “Where are you?”” 3:9 (NLT) God is not searching because He has lost them. The question invites them to come out and say it. He came near, and He spoke first.

Their answers never reach the end of themselves. “It was the woman you gave me who gave me the fruit, and I ate it” 3:12 (NLT). Deception was real. So was pressure. But there is a line between explaining and owning, and God’s questions walk us to it. Ellen G. White saw the habit: “Instead of humbly confessing their sins, they try to shield themselves by casting the blame upon others, upon circumstances, or upon God—making even His blessings an occasion of murmuring against Him.” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 58 · PP 58.1

The first people hide, but the first movement of grace is God coming near.

God speaks judgment, and does not soften it. But before one word about pain or thorns or dust comes a promise: “And I will cause hostility between you and the woman, and between your offspring and her offspring. He will strike your head, and you will strike his heel” 3:15 (NLT). Hope arrives first. Ellen G. White points at the order: “This sentence, uttered in the hearing of our first parents, was to them a promise. While it foretold war between man and Satan, it declared that the power of the great adversary would finally be broken.” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 65 · PP 65.4

Then come the hard words, which we must not hurry past: “For you were made from dust, and to dust you will return” 3:19 (NLT, excerpt). Yet mercy appears inside the judgment. God makes them something better than leaves: “And the LORD God made clothing from animal skins for Adam and his wife” 3:21 (NLT). They leave the garden, and the way back to the tree of life is guarded. But no one is shut forever inside a ruined life.

“Christ’s one act of righteousness brings a right relationship with God and new life for everyone” Romans 5:18 (NLT, excerpt). The serpent’s head is struck by the One who was struck for us. So come out of the trees. Bring God the true sentence, not the edited one. If what you carry involves harm or danger, or is too heavy to hold alone, tell a trustworthy pastor or counsellor and get safe help.

God still asks, “Where are you?” — not because He cannot find us, but because grace is calling us to stop hiding and come home.

Reflect

  1. Where am I letting a distorted picture of God make disobedience look reasonable?
  2. What am I hiding from God that He is lovingly calling into the light?
  3. Where have I been explaining a choice that I need to start owning?

Key Takeaways

  1. Temptation goes after trust before it asks for our hands. “Did God really say you must not eat the fruit from any of the trees in the garden?” Genesis 3:1 (NLT, excerpt) The question moved the fence before anyone touched the fruit. Ellen G. White: “He tempts men to distrust God’s love and to doubt His wisdom.” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 54 · PP 54.3
  2. Shame builds coverings; repentance walks back. “So they sewed fig leaves together to cover themselves” 3:7 (NLT, excerpt), and then “they hid from the LORD God among the trees” 3:8 (NLT, excerpt). Hiding was the first instinct, not returning.
  3. God came looking, and He spoke first. “Then the LORD God called to the man, “Where are you?”” 3:9 (NLT) His question is not ignorance; it is an invitation to come out and tell the truth without “casting the blame upon others, upon circumstances, or upon God” Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 58 · PP 58.1.
  4. Hope was spoken before the sentence was finished. “He will strike your head, and you will strike his heel” 3:15 (NLT, excerpt) came before a single word about pain, thorns or dust — and “Christ’s one act of righteousness brings a right relationship with God and new life for everyone” Romans 5:18 (NLT, excerpt).

Sources and Further ReadingScripture: Genesis 3:1–24, with the key text at 3:9 and the hope text at 3:15, read with Genesis 2:15–17 and 2:25 as background and Romans 5:12–19 as the New Testament connection. Quoted on this page: Genesis 2:15, 16, 17, 25; 3:1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 12, 13, 14, 15, 19, 21; Romans 5:18 — all NLT, with every excerpt marked. Genesis never names the fruit or the kind of tree, and neither does this page. Genesis 3:15 is spoken inside God’s judgment on the serpent: it promises conflict and the serpent’s final defeat, and it does not contain Jesus’ name or an explanation of the cross. Christian readers see its fulfilment through the later witness of Scripture, especially Romans 5:12–19, and that connection is named here as canonical interpretation rather than as the wording of Genesis. Genesis 3:21 records that God made the clothing and says nothing about sacrifice or atonement. The NLT footnote at 3:15 gives “bruise” as an alternative to “strike” in both halves of the verse, and the footnote at 3:16 gives “And though you will have desire for your husband, / he will rule over you.” Ellen G. White: Patriarchs and Prophets, chapter 3, “The Temptation and Fall,” pp. 52–62, quoted at pp. 54, 55, 57 and 58 (PP 54.3, 55.2, 57.1, 58.1); chapter 4, “The Plan of Redemption,” pp. 63–70, quoted at p. 65 (PP 65.4). She tells us what temptation is actually aiming at, where the fall began, what was lost inside them, what self-defence does to confession, and that the sentence on the serpent came to the hiding pair as a promise.
