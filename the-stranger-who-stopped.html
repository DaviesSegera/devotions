The Stranger Who Stopped

Luke 10:25–37 · 10 min read

By Dr. Davies Rene Segera · 2026-11-14

Two men pass by a wounded traveler, but a Samaritan stops to care for him. Reflect on what it means to be a neighbor and how the mercy Jesus shows us moves us to help others.

Love & serviceForgiveness & graceHumility & obedience
Watch the Short

Key Verse
“Then a despised Samaritan came along, and when he saw the man, he felt compassion for him.”
Luke 10:33 (NLT)

Have you ever asked a question and hoped the answer would let you carry on as before? A teacher of God’s law once asked Jesus about eternal life. By the end of their conversation, Jesus had asked him to consider what it means to love a neighbor.

“One day an expert in religious law stood up to test Jesus by asking him this question: ‘Teacher, what should I do to inherit eternal life?’” Luke 10:25, NLT Jesus directed him back to the law. “What does the law of Moses say? How do you read it?” Luke 10:26, NLT, excerpt

The teacher drew his answer from two commandments. “‘You must love the Lord your God with all your heart, all your soul, all your strength, and all your mind.’ And, ‘Love your neighbor as yourself.’” Luke 10:27, NLT, excerpt Jesus agreed with him. “‘Right!’ Jesus told him. ‘Do this and you will live!’” Luke 10:28, NLT

Luke tells us that the man came to test Jesus. Ellen G. White adds that he sincerely wanted to understand what he had been studying. “He had been studying the scriptures with a desire to learn their real meaning. He had a vital interest in the matter, and he asked in sincerity, ‘What shall I do?’” Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 377 · COL 377.3, excerpt

The man already knew the right answer. Jesus wanted him to live it.

The teacher had another question. “The man wanted to justify his actions, so he asked Jesus, ‘And who is my neighbor?’” Luke 10:29, NLT He was looking for a way to defend his own conduct. Ellen G. White explains what lay behind his question. “Rather than acknowledge the truth, he sought to show how difficult of fulfillment the commandment is.” The Desire of Ages, p. 498 · DA 498.2, excerpt

“Who is my neighbor?” sounds like a reasonable question. We might ask it ourselves. Jesus answered with a story that brought the question closer to home.

“But by a simple story He held up before His hearers such a picture of the outflowing of heaven-born love as touched all hearts, and drew from the lawyer a confession of the truth.”The Desire of Ages, p. 498 · DA 498.4, excerpt

Sometimes we ask who our neighbor is because we want to know where our duty ends.

“Jesus replied with a story: ‘A Jewish man was traveling from Jerusalem down to Jericho, and he was attacked by bandits. They stripped him of his clothes, beat him up, and left him half dead beside the road.’” Luke 10:30, NLT

Then two men came along, each with a chance to help. “By chance a priest came along. But when he saw the man lying there, he crossed to the other side of the road and passed him by.” Luke 10:31, NLT “A Temple assistant walked over and looked at him lying there, but he also passed by on the other side.” Luke 10:32, NLT The “Temple assistant” was a Levite, a member of the tribe that served at the Temple. He and the priest both had duties in God’s house.

Luke leaves their reasons unstated. Ellen G. White describes the Levite’s struggle.

“He was convicted of what he ought to do; but it was not an agreeable duty. He wished that he had not come that way, so that he need not have seen the wounded man. He persuaded himself that the case was no concern of his.”The Desire of Ages, p. 499 · DA 499.2, excerpt

Many of us know how quickly we can find a reason to keep walking when we see someone in need.

Both men saw the wounded man, and both chose to keep walking.

Then came the last person Jesus’ listeners would have expected to stop. “Then a despised Samaritan came along, and when he saw the man, he felt compassion for him.” Luke 10:33, NLT Jews and Samaritans had long been divided. They shared some beliefs and differed in how they worshipped. John’s Gospel gives us a glimpse of how deep that divide ran. “The woman was surprised, for Jews refuse to have anything to do with Samaritans.” John 4:9, NLT, excerpt

The Samaritan made the wounded man’s needs his concern. “Going over to him, the Samaritan soothed his wounds with olive oil and wine and bandaged them. Then he put the man on his own donkey and took him to an inn, where he took care of him.” Luke 10:34, NLT “The next day he handed the innkeeper two silver coins, telling him, ‘Take care of this man. If his bill runs higher than this, I’ll pay you the next time I’m here.’” Luke 10:35, NLT

Those two silver coins amounted to about two days’ wages. The Samaritan gave his time, used his own supplies and took responsibility for the man’s care even after he left the inn. Ellen G. White points out the risk of tarrying, or staying, on the road. “He did not consider that he himself might be in danger of violence by tarrying in the place. It was enough that there was before him a human being in need and suffering.” The Desire of Ages, p. 503 · DA 503.2, excerpt

The Samaritan let compassion interrupt his journey.

After the story, Jesus turned the question back to the teacher. “Now which of these three would you say was a neighbor to the man who was attacked by bandits?” Luke 10:36, NLT, excerpt The teacher had asked who counted as his neighbor. Jesus asked which man had acted as a neighbor. “The man replied, ‘The one who showed him mercy.’ Then Jesus said, ‘Yes, now go and do the same.’” Luke 10:37, NLT

The law the teacher knew so well already called the people of Israel to love the foreigners living among them. “Treat them like native-born Israelites, and love them as you love yourself. Remember that you were once foreigners living in the land of Egypt. I am the LORD your God.” Leviticus 19:34, NLT Ellen G. White sums up Jesus’ answer.

“Thus the question, ‘Who is my neighbor?’ is forever answered. Christ has shown that our neighbor does not mean merely one of the church or faith to which we belong. It has no reference to race, color, or class distinction. Our neighbor is every person who needs our help. Our neighbor is every soul who is wounded and bruised by the adversary. Our neighbor is everyone who is the property of God.”The Desire of Ages, p. 503 · DA 503.5

“The adversary” is Satan, the enemy of our souls. Anyone who needs our help is our neighbor, whatever their background or belief.

Jesus calls us to show mercy wherever we find someone in need.

Christians have long heard in this parable a call to show compassion. Many have also seen in it a picture of Christ’s saving work. Ellen G. White holds these two themes together.

“In the story of the good Samaritan, Jesus gave a picture of Himself and His mission. Man had been deceived, bruised, robbed, and ruined by Satan, and left to perish; but the Saviour had compassion on our helpless condition. He left His glory, to come to our rescue. He found us ready to die, and He undertook our case. He healed our wounds. He covered us with His robe of righteousness. He opened to us a refuge of safety, and made complete provision for us at His own charges. He died to redeem us.”The Desire of Ages, p. 503 · DA 503.6, excerpt

Christ’s robe of righteousness is His own goodness, which He gives to cover us. “At His own charges” means at His own cost.

The Samaritan crossed a deep divide to help a stranger. Paul tells us how far Christ went to reach us. “But God showed his great love for us by sending Christ to die for us while we were still sinners.” Romans 5:8, NLT Ellen G. White adds, “He died to save His enemies. He prayed for His murderers.” Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 381 · COL 381.1, excerpt

Before Jesus asked us to stop for others, He stopped for us.

How do we learn to show that kind of mercy? Ellen G. White points us back to Christ.

“Christ knew that no one could obey the law in his own strength. He desired to lead the lawyer to clearer and more critical research that he might find the truth. Only by accepting the virtue and grace of Christ can we keep the law. Belief in the propitiation for sin enables fallen man to love God with his whole heart and his neighbor as himself.”Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 378 · COL 378.1

Here, propitiation refers to Christ’s sacrifice, which takes away our sin and makes peace between us and God. Paul reminds us that salvation is a gift we receive by grace. “God saved you by his grace when you believed. And you can’t take credit for this; it is a gift from God.” Ephesians 2:8, NLT By that same grace, God gives us a new way to live. “He has created us anew in Christ Jesus, so we can do the good things he planned for us long ago.” Ephesians 2:10, NLT, excerpt

John tells us where this love begins. “We love each other because he loved us first.” 1 John 4:19, NLT Ellen G. White describes what happens when we receive that love. “What is needed is the love of Christ in the heart. When self is merged in Christ, love springs forth spontaneously.” Christ’s Object Lessons, p. 384 · COL 384.2, excerpt As we give ourselves to Christ, His love shapes the way we respond to others.

Being a neighbor might mean visiting someone, bringing a meal, making a phone call or finding someone who can help safely. “Dear children, let’s not merely say that we love each other; let us show the truth by our actions.” 1 John 3:18, NLT This week, we may meet someone who needs us to stop.

Jesus stopped for us. Now He sends us to do the same for someone else.

Reflect

  1. Whose needs have I quietly dismissed?
  2. What do I tell myself when I’m tempted to pass by?
  3. How does remembering that Jesus stopped for me change the way I see someone who needs my help?

Key Takeaways

  1. Be a neighbor. Jesus calls us to follow the Samaritan’s example and show mercy to the person in need. Luke 10:36–37 · quoted above
  2. Compassion stops and comes close. The Samaritan tended the man’s wounds, brought him to safety and paid for his care. Luke 10:34–35 · quoted above
  3. Jesus stopped for us first. He loved us while we were still sinners, and His love gives us the strength to care for others. Romans 5:8 · quoted above

White, The Desire of Ages, ch. 54, “The Good Samaritan,” pp. 497–505 (DA 498.2, 498.4, 499.2, 503.2, 503.5, 503.6); Christ’s Object Lessons, ch. 27, “Who is My Neighbour?,” pp. 376–389 (COL 377.3, 378.1, 381.1, 384.2).

Translation note: In Luke 10:32 the NLT’s “Temple assistant” translates the Greek word for a Levite, and in Luke 10:35 “two silver coins” translates “two denarii,” each about a labourer’s full day’s wage (NLT footnotes). In Luke 10:30 the NLT’s “A Jewish man” makes explicit what the Greek gives as “a certain man.”

A prayer for today

Heavenly Father, thank You for sending Jesus to us when we were wounded and helpless. He came near, healed us and paid the cost Himself. Forgive us for the times we’ve seen someone in need and kept walking. Forgive us for keeping our distance from people who are different from us. Fill our hearts with Christ’s love so we can stop for those who are hurting. Give us the wisdom to know how to help, the courage to draw near and the kindness to speak gently. Show each of us one person we can care for this week. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
