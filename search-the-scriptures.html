Today's Devotion

Search the Scriptures

Acts 17 · 20 min read
Key Verse
“And the people of Berea were more open-minded than those in Thessalonica, and they listened eagerly to Paul’s message. They searched the Scriptures day after day to see if Paul and Silas were teaching the truth.”
Acts 17:11 (NLT)

Luke does not begin the story of the Bereans in Berea. He begins one city earlier, in Thessalonica, and he begins with a preacher who opened the Scriptures before anyone thought to open them on him. “Paul and Silas then traveled through the towns of Amphipolis and Apollonia and came to Thessalonica, where there was a Jewish synagogue.” Acts 17:1 (NLT) “As was Paul’s custom, he went to the synagogue service, and for three Sabbaths in a row he used the Scriptures to reason with the people. He explained the prophecies and proved that the Messiah must suffer and rise from the dead. He said, ‘This Jesus I’m telling you about is the Messiah.’” Acts 17:2–3 (NLT)

Notice what Paul did not do. He did not ask the synagogue to trust his credentials, his conversion story or his eloquence. He reasoned from the Scriptures, and the subject of his reasoning was Christ: a suffering, rising Messiah, and the announcement that Jesus was that Messiah. This is the same method the risen Jesus had used on the Emmaus road, when He “took them through the writings of Moses and all the prophets, explaining from all the Scriptures the things concerning himself.” Luke 24:27 (NLT)

Ellen White observes that Paul “appealed to the scriptures foretelling the birth, sufferings, death, resurrection, and ascension of Christ,” and that “by the inspired testimony of Moses and the prophets he clearly proved the identity of Jesus of Nazareth with the Messiah” The Acts of the Apostles, chapter 22, “Thessalonica,” p. 221 · AA 221.2. She adds that he “exalted Christ, the proper understanding of whose ministry is the key that unlocks the Old Testament Scriptures” The Acts of the Apostles, chapter 22, p. 229 · AA 229.1.

Hold on to that before we reach Berea. The message the Bereans would later examine was not a rival to Scripture. It was an argument built from Scripture, about Christ.

Paul opened the Scriptures before anyone opened them on him, and the message he preached was Scripture’s own testimony to Jesus.

Luke records two responses in Thessalonica, and it matters that we keep both. First: “Some of the Jews who listened were persuaded and joined Paul and Silas, along with many God-fearing Greek men and quite a few prominent women.” Acts 17:4 (NLT) A church was born in that city, and Paul later thanked God that when the Thessalonians “received his message from us, you didn’t think of our words as mere human ideas. You accepted what we said as the very word of God.” 1 Thessalonians 2:13 (NLT)

Whatever Luke goes on to say about Berea, it is not a verdict on every Thessalonian. Second: “But some of the Jews were jealous, so they gathered some troublemakers from the marketplace to form a mob and start a riot. They attacked the home of Jason, searching for Paul and Silas so they could drag them out to the crowd.” Acts 17:5 (NLT) Not finding them, they dragged Jason and other believers before the city council and charged them with treason: “They are all guilty of treason against Caesar, for they profess allegiance to another king, named Jesus.” Acts 17:7 (NLT)

Luke is precise about who did this: “some of the Jews” Acts 17:5 (NLT), not the Jewish people, and some of the very people who believed in verse 4 were Jews. The issue in Acts 17 is never ethnicity. It is the posture of a heart towards truth. Jason and the others were forced to post bond, and then Luke writes the sentence that carries the story into Berea: “That very night the believers sent Paul and Silas to Berea. When they arrived there, they went to the Jewish synagogue.” Acts 17:10 (NLT)

Driven out in the dark, Paul did in Berea exactly what he had done in Thessalonica. He went to the synagogue and opened the Scriptures again. Ellen White summarises it in one line: “Fearing further violence, ‘the brethren immediately sent away Paul and Silas by night unto Berea’” The Acts of the Apostles, chapter 22, p. 230 · AA 230.1, quoting the King James Version.

Opposition changed Paul’s address, but it did not change his method: in the next town he went straight back to the Scriptures.

Now comes the verse that names this devotion. “And the people of Berea were more open-minded than those in Thessalonica, and they listened eagerly to Paul’s message. They searched the Scriptures day after day to see if Paul and Silas were teaching the truth.” Acts 17:11 (NLT) A word about wording, because this verse is often quoted from memory in a mixture of translations. The NLT says the Bereans were “more open-minded”; the King James Version, which Ellen White quotes, says they were “more noble … in that they received the word with all readiness of mind, and searched the Scriptures daily, whether those things were so.”

The NLT’s “more open-minded” and the King James Version’s “more noble” translate the same Greek word, and this devotion quotes each version in its own place without blending them. What both make plain is that Luke commends two things at once, and he refuses to separate them. The Bereans listened eagerly. And the Bereans searched. Ellen White puts it this way: “The minds of the Bereans were not narrowed by prejudice. They were willing to investigate the truthfulness of the doctrines preached by the apostles” The Acts of the Apostles, chapter 23, “Berea and Athens,” p. 231 · AA 231.2.

Look at what their response was not. It was not blind acceptance; they searched. It was not automatic rejection; they listened eagerly. It was not indifference; they did it day after day. And it was not hostile suspicion; Luke calls them open-minded, not guarded. Many of us default to one side of that verse. Some hearts are so open that anything confident and well delivered gets in. Some minds are so guarded that nothing unfamiliar is ever weighed fairly. The Bereans did not choose between an open heart and a discerning mind. They practised both. An open Bible accompanied an open mind.

They welcomed the message eagerly, but they gave Scripture the final word.

What exactly did the Bereans search? Not a bound New Testament; none existed yet, and the writings that would become the New Testament were still being written. “The Scriptures” in a first-century synagogue meant the sacred writings Israel already possessed: the law of Moses, the prophets and the psalms, the very books Jesus had named when He said, “everything written about me in the law of Moses and the prophets and in the Psalms must be fulfilled.” Luke 24:44 (NLT) That is worth pausing on.

The Bereans could test Paul’s claims about a crucified and risen Messiah because those Scriptures already testified to Him. Jesus had said as much to religious leaders who studied hard and missed Him: “You search the Scriptures because you think they give you eternal life. But the Scriptures point to me!” John 5:39 (NLT) The Bereans searched the same Scriptures and found the One they point to. Ellen White describes the purpose and the manner of their study:

“They studied the Bible, not from curiosity, but in order that they might learn what had been written concerning the promised Messiah. Daily they searched the inspired records, and as they compared scripture with scripture, heavenly angels were beside them, enlightening their minds and impressing their hearts”. The Acts of the Apostles, chapter 23, p. 231 · AA 231.2

Luke records the daily searching and its purpose; Ellen White lets us see more of what that searching looked like, scripture compared with scripture, and heaven itself drawing near to those who study. The principle stands out clearly in both: this was serious study with a serious purpose. It was not curiosity, not a hunt for something novel, not the enjoyment of a good argument. It was people wanting to know whether the Messiah had come. And it was daily. The Bereans did not treat Scripture as an emergency reference to be consulted when a preacher said something alarming.

They returned to it day after day, letting it shape their thinking while the question was still open. The psalmist knew why: “Your word is a lamp to guide my feet and a light for my path.” Psalm 119:105 (NLT) “The teaching of your word gives light, so even the simple can understand.” Psalm 119:130 (NLT) Daily Bible study earns nothing from God; it is not a payment. It is the place where God’s light reaches an honest mind.

The Bereans opened their minds to the message because they had already opened their Scriptures, and they were searching for Christ, not for a debate.

Consider who the Bereans were examining. Not a novice, not a stranger to the Scriptures, not a false teacher. An apostle of Jesus Christ, personally called by the risen Lord, whose message was true. And they still searched the Scriptures to see whether it was so. Luke does not record that Paul was offended. He records that many believed. Far from resenting the practice, Paul taught it. To the church in Thessalonica, the city he had just been driven from, he wrote: “Do not scoff at prophecies, but test everything that is said. Hold on to what is good.” 1 Thessalonians 5:20–21 (NLT)

John gave the same instruction: “Dear friends, do not believe everyone who claims to speak by the Spirit. You must test them to see if the spirit they have comes from God. For there are many false prophets in the world.” 1 John 4:1 (NLT) And Isaiah had set the standard centuries before: “Look to God’s instructions and teachings! People who contradict his word are completely in the dark.” Isaiah 8:20 (NLT) The principle is simple to state and hard to keep. Charisma is not the final authority.

Popularity is not the final authority. Tradition is not the final authority. A preacher’s confidence, a viral sermon, a denominational label, our own feelings and our own preferences are not the final authority. God’s revealed Word is. But here is where a Berean spirit must be distinguished from something that merely looks like it. Biblical discernment is not cynical suspicion. It is a humble willingness to test what we hear by the Word of God. The Bereans were not sitting in the synagogue hoping to catch Paul in an error; they were listening eagerly, hoping he was right, and going to the Scriptures to find out.

Scripture honours faithful teachers. It calls pastors and teachers gifts Christ gave to His church, whose “responsibility is to equip God’s people to do his work and build up the church, the body of Christ.” Ephesians 4:12 (NLT) It says, “Remember your leaders who taught you the word of God. Think of all the good that has come from their lives, and follow the example of their faith.” Hebrews 13:7 (NLT) A church that treats its pastor as an adversary has not become Berean; it has become something Luke never commends.

A Berean church is not a church where everyone distrusts everyone else. It is a church where preacher and congregation together willingly submit to the authority of God’s Word.

Testing a teacher by Scripture is not an act of distrust towards the teacher; it is an act of trust in the Word that both of you are under.

Ellen White’s strongest words on this passage concern prejudice, and they cut in more than one direction. Of the Bereans she writes that their minds “were not narrowed by prejudice,” and then she turns to her readers:

“Wherever the truths of the gospel are proclaimed, those who honestly desire to do right are led to a diligent searching of the Scriptures. … But when unpopular Bible truths are presented, many refuse to make this investigation. Though unable to controvert the plain teachings of Scripture, they yet manifest the utmost reluctance to study the evidences offered. Some assume that even if these doctrines are indeed true, it matters little whether or not they accept the new light, and they cling to pleasing fables which the enemy uses to lead souls astray”. The Acts of the Apostles, chapter 23, p. 232 · AA 232.1

The danger she names is not believing the wrong thing after honest study. It is refusing to study at all because we already know what we want the answer to be. Prejudice wears many clothes. We reject a biblical teaching because we dislike the speaker, or accept one because we admire him. We refuse to reconsider what we inherited. We let a denominational rivalry, a political loyalty or a cultural instinct settle a question before the Bible has been opened. We read only what confirms us.

We treat every sincere question as a threat. Jesus tied understanding to willingness: “Anyone who wants to do the will of God will know whether my teaching is from God or is merely my own.” John 7:17 (NLT) That is why the Bible should correct us more often than we use it merely to confirm us. But pastoral balance is needed here, and Scripture supplies it. Not every longstanding belief is wrong; Paul told Timothy to “remain faithful to the things you have been taught.” 2 Timothy 3:14 (NLT)

Not every new interpretation is right; John warned that “there are many false prophets in the world.” 1 John 4:1 (NLT) Novelty does not prove truth. Age does not prove truth. Majority and minority, popularity and unpopularity prove nothing on their own. The Berean question is never “Is this old?” or “Is this new?” but “Is this so?” and the willingness to say: show me from Scripture, and if God’s Word teaches this, I will follow it. Truth does not fear careful biblical examination. That does not mean every question is settled quickly or that sincere believers will never differ; some passages ask for patience, prayer and humility. It means that no honest question, taken to Scripture, will harm the truth.

A teachable Christian does not reject truth because it is unfamiliar, and does not accept teaching merely because it sounds convincing; the standard is neither old nor new, but Scripture.

The story does not end with “they investigated.” Luke writes: “As a result, many Jews believed, as did many of the prominent Greek women and men.” Acts 17:12 (NLT) Three things stand out. First, the searching produced faith. The Bereans were not commended for collecting information; they were open, they investigated, and when the Scriptures showed that Paul was teaching the truth about Jesus, they believed. Second, the believers were Jews and Greeks together, and Luke goes out of his way to name the prominent Greek women alongside the men.

The same Scriptures, examined honestly, brought people of different backgrounds and standing to the same Christ. Third, this was faith produced by the Word about Christ: “So faith comes from hearing, that is, hearing the Good News about Christ.” Romans 10:17 (NLT) Here is a searching question for anyone who loves Bible study. What is the value of discovering truth if I refuse to follow it? There is a kind of investigation that goes on forever precisely so that it never has to commit; every answer produces another objection, and “I am still studying” becomes a permanent address.

That is not the Berean spirit. The Bereans studied until the matter was clear, and then they responded. James describes the alternative: “But don’t just listen to God’s word. You must do what it says. Otherwise, you are only fooling yourselves.” James 1:22 (NLT) Ellen White writes, of the very next city Paul visited, a sentence that explains why searching alone was never enough: “No eloquence of words, no force of argument, can convert the sinner. The power of God alone can apply the truth to the heart” The Acts of the Apostles, chapter 23, p. 239 · AA 239.3.

She wrote that of Athens, where Luke records that “some laughed in contempt, but others said, ‘We want to hear more about this later.’” Acts 17:32 (NLT) A few believed there. What she says of Athens is just as true of Berea. The Bereans’ diligence did not save them. God, through His Word, brought them to faith in His Son, and they did not refuse it. Bible study that terminates in knowledge has stopped one step short. Truth calls for a response.

The Bereans did not search Scripture merely to collect information; their investigation led many of them to faith, because the Scriptures they searched point to Christ.

Then the trouble that had followed Paul out of one city followed him into the next. “But when some Jews in Thessalonica learned that Paul was preaching the word of God in Berea, they went there and stirred up trouble.” Acts 17:13 (NLT) Again Luke says “some.” “The believers acted at once, sending Paul on to the coast, while Silas and Timothy remained behind. Those escorting Paul went with him all the way to Athens; then they returned to Berea with instructions for Silas and Timothy to hurry and join him.” Acts 17:14–15 (NLT)

Ellen White reads the scene the same way: the opponents

“followed them to Berea and aroused against them the excitable passions of the lower class. Fearing that violence would be done to Paul if he remained there, the brethren sent him to Athens, accompanied by some of the Bereans who had newly accepted the faith”. The Acts of the Apostles, chapter 23, p. 232 · AA 232.3

She adds a touching detail: the escort was made up of new Berean believers, people who had received the Word only days before and were already serving it. She continues: “The enemies of Christ could not prevent the advancement of the gospel, but they succeeded in making the work of the apostles exceedingly hard” The Acts of the Apostles, chapter 23, p. 233 · AA 233.1. And of the two who stayed she writes that Timothy “with Silas had remained to carry on the work so well begun there, and to instruct the new converts in the principles of the faith” The Acts of the Apostles, chapter 23, p. 233 · AA 233.3.

Luke tells us that Silas and Timothy remained; the purpose of their staying is her reasonable reading of it. Weigh what the opposition achieved. It removed a messenger. It did not remove the message. The Scriptures were still in Berea. The believers were still in Berea. Silas and Timothy were still in Berea. And the Word that had produced faith did not leave with Paul. There is a difference between a messenger being moved on and the message of God being defeated, and Acts 17 shows only the first.

Two cautions belong here. Opposition is not proof that a teaching is true; false teaching meets resistance too, and the standard remains Scripture. And not every disagreement is persecution; a church that hears every criticism as an attack has lost the Berean posture of listening eagerly.

Opposition could move the preacher out of town, but it could not move the Word out of the hearts that had searched it and believed.

So what would happen if our church received God’s Word eagerly while also testing every teaching carefully by Scripture? Picture the congregation. Members bring their Bibles, and open them. They read the passage for themselves, in context, rather than taking a verse on trust. They ask sincere questions and are not treated as troublemakers for asking. They listen carefully and respectfully to faithful teachers without treating any teacher as infallible, and they pray for understanding rather than assuming that cleverness alone will supply it. They refuse gossip, theological sensationalism and personality-driven Christianity.

They are willing to learn, willing to correct error, and willing to be corrected. They study through the week instead of living on one sermon, and they let Scripture reach their decisions, their money, their relationships and their behaviour. And when the truth becomes clear, they follow it. The healthiest congregation is not one in which the pastor does all the Bible study for the members, but one in which the preaching sends the members back to Scripture for themselves. That does not undermine pastors; it is what faithful pastors want.

Ellen White writes: “Those who are sincerely seeking for truth will make a careful investigation, in the light of God’s word, of the doctrines presented to them” The Acts of the Apostles, chapter 23, p. 232 · AA 232.2. But keep the centre in view, because the Bereans did. They were not examining abstract doctrine; they were examining a message about Jesus, and the Scriptures they searched are the Scriptures that “have given you the wisdom to receive the salvation that comes by trusting in Christ Jesus.” 2 Timothy 3:15 (NLT) “All Scripture is inspired by God and is useful to teach us what is true and to make us realize what is wrong in our lives. It corrects us when we are wrong and teaches us to do what is right.” 2 Timothy 3:16 (NLT)

The goal of searching Scripture was never to become a skilled critic of sermons. It was to discover what God has actually said, and what God has said is Christ. Ask yourself honestly: Do I open the Bible to discover truth, or mainly to defend what I already believe? If Scripture showed me that a cherished assumption was wrong, would I be willing to change? Is my Bible study producing obedience and faith, or merely information? The Bereans heard an apostle preach, and then they went to the Word and found Jesus there. Receive the Word eagerly. Search the Scriptures carefully. Follow the truth faithfully.

Genuine faith does not require us to choose between openness and discernment; the Bereans listened eagerly, searched daily, believed in Christ, and call us to be better followers of Jesus rather than merely better critics of sermons.

Key Takeaways

  1. Paul opened the Scriptures first. “He used the Scriptures to reason with the people. He explained the prophecies and proved that the Messiah must suffer and rise from the dead.” Acts 17:2–3 (NLT) The message the Bereans tested was itself built from Scripture, about Christ; a faithful sermon should never replace your Bible, it should send you back to it.
  2. They welcomed the message eagerly, but they gave Scripture the final word. “They listened eagerly to Paul’s message. They searched the Scriptures day after day to see if Paul and Silas were teaching the truth.” Acts 17:11 (NLT) Not blind acceptance, not automatic rejection, not indifference, not suspicion. An open Bible accompanied an open mind.
  3. They searched day after day, not once in a crisis. “Your word is a lamp to guide my feet and a light for my path.” Psalm 119:105 (NLT) Ellen White writes that they “studied the Bible, not from curiosity, but in order that they might learn what had been written concerning the promised Messiah” The Acts of the Apostles, chapter 23, p. 231 · AA 231.2. Daily study earns nothing; it is where God’s light reaches an honest mind.
  4. They were not controlled by prejudice. Ellen White says their minds “were not narrowed by prejudice,” and warns that “when unpopular Bible truths are presented, many refuse to make this investigation” The Acts of the Apostles, chapter 23, pp. 231–232 · AA 231.2; AA 232.1. “Anyone who wants to do the will of God will know whether my teaching is from God or is merely my own.” John 7:17 (NLT) Neither age nor novelty proves truth; the Bible should correct us more often than we use it to confirm us.
  5. Discernment is not suspicion. “Test everything that is said. Hold on to what is good.” 1 Thessalonians 5:21 (NLT) Pastors and teachers are gifts whose “responsibility is to equip God’s people to do his work.” Ephesians 4:12 (NLT) A Berean church is not one where everyone distrusts everyone else, but one where preacher and congregation together submit to God’s Word.
  6. Their search led to faith. “As a result, many Jews believed, as did many of the prominent Greek women and men.” Acts 17:12 (NLT) “Don’t just listen to God’s word. You must do what it says.” James 1:22 (NLT) What is the value of discovering truth if I refuse to follow it? Bible study that ends in information has stopped one step short.
  7. Opposition moved the messenger, not the message. “The believers acted at once, sending Paul on to the coast, while Silas and Timothy remained behind.” Acts 17:14 (NLT) Ellen White writes that “the enemies of Christ could not prevent the advancement of the gospel” The Acts of the Apostles, chapter 23, p. 233 · AA 233.1. The Scriptures point to Christ, so receive the Word eagerly, search the Scriptures carefully, and follow the truth faithfully.
